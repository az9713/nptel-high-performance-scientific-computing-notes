The components of an HPC system and the architecture of a compute node

A job needs \(10^{15}\) floating-point operations, one petaflop of work. Written as a plain loop and compiled without vectorization, it runs at about 9 Gflop/s on one core of the machine used in this chapter, and the job takes about 30 hours. The same loop compiled for the instructions that the core has (vector registers and fused multiply-add) runs at about 59 Gflop/s on the same core, and the job takes 4.7 hours, a factor of 6.2 less. The node has 8 cores and 12 hardware threads; the highest rate measured on all of them with a register-resident multiply-add kernel is 284 Gflop/s (196 to 284 Gflop/s in eight runs), which would take the job 0.98 hours. The machine that the lecture presents as the largest, Frontier, reached 1.206 Eflop/s on the Linpack benchmark in June 2024, which is \(10^{15}\) operations in 0.83 ms. This chapter defines the parts of such a system (login nodes, compute nodes, interconnect, parallel file system, scheduler), derives the peak rate of one node from the structure of its cores, and measures how far ordinary code stays below that peak and which hardware features it leaves unused.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The chapter studies one question at two scales: how fast can a machine compute, and what in its structure sets that limit. The objects are defined here, bottom up, and each is used with this meaning throughout.

Processors. A floating-point operation (flop) is one addition, subtraction or multiplication of two double-precision numbers (64-bit floating-point numbers). A fused multiply-add (FMA) computes \(a\cdot b+c\) as one instruction and counts as 2 flops. A core is a unit that fetches and executes the instructions of one instruction stream. A core runs at a clock frequency \(f\) in cycles per second; one cycle is \(1/f\) seconds. A hardware thread (a logical processor) is a set of architectural state (registers, program counter) in a core that holds one instruction stream; the operating system schedules software threads onto logical processors, and a core has one or two of them on the machines of this chapter. A socket holds one processor chip, which has one or more cores; a node is one computer with one or more sockets, memory, input and output devices, and a network connection. A cluster is a set of nodes joined by a network and used together.

The rate of one core. A vector register holds \(\ell\) doubles (\(\ell=4\) for a 256-bit register), and a vector instruction applies one operation to all \(\ell\) lanes. Let \(X\) be the throughput of FMA instructions, the largest number of FMA instructions that the core can start per cycle, and \(L\) their latency, the number of cycles from the moment the operands are ready to the moment the result is ready. The peak rate of a core is

\[ \phi = 2\,X\,\ell\ \text{flop per cycle},\qquad P_{\text{core}}=f\,\phi . \tag{6.1} \]

A node of \(c\) identical cores has \(P_{\text{node}}=c\,f\,\phi\), and a cluster of \(n_{\text{node}}\) such nodes has \(P_{\text{peak}}=n_{\text{node}}\,c\,f\,\phi\). This theoretical peak, \(R_{\text{peak}}\) in the vocabulary of the TOP500 list, is a product of hardware parameters. It is not a property of any program. The rate that a program reaches is a different quantity, and the ratio of the two is the efficiency of the program on that machine. The quantity \(R_{\max}\) is the rate that one named program, the Linpack benchmark, reached (next section).

The machine of this chapter. Every measurement comes from one laptop: an Intel Core i5-12450H under Windows 11, with all programs built and run in a WSL 2 Ubuntu 24.04 guest by gcc 13.3. The running case is the arithmetic of one node. Chapter 9 defines the book's Jacobi problem, and chapter 10 asks whether its sweep is limited by flops or by bytes; this chapter asks the earlier question, what the flop limit of one node is, and uses a polynomial loop that stays in the L1 cache so that memory does not enter. Units, the TOP500 list, and what the lecture means by high performance computing W2L3 0:06

History and the speed plot. The lecture begins with a photograph of ENIAC on a slide titled "ENIAC – Circa 1947". The 1961 monograph of the US Army Ordnance Corps on its electronic computers states that final assembly took place in the fall of 1945, that ENIAC was formally dedicated at the Moore School of the University of Pennsylvania on 15 February 1946, that the Ordnance Department accepted it in July 1946, that its first units arrived at Aberdeen Proving Ground in January 1947 and that it became operational again there by August 1947 (US Army Ordnance, Electronic Computers within the Ordnance Corps, 1961, chapter II, pages 23 to 24 of the monograph). The sponsor in that account is the Ordnance Department of the Army, and the purpose it names is the preparation of firing tables. The slide's "circa 1947" is therefore consistent with the machine at Aberdeen, which was operating there in 1947, while the dedication was a year earlier. The photograph on the slide shows the plugboards and cables of the units; the monograph describes the plugging of connections that set the sequence of operations.

The lecture's first plot shows the speed of the fastest computers against the year on a logarithmic axis, from one operation per second in about 1938 to about \(10^{18}\) in 2022, with integer operations per second before about 1958 and floating-point operations per second afterwards. The slide labels the vertical axis "Peak speed (Rmax)" and cites Wikipedia. The label joins two quantities that the TOP500 list keeps apart, and the next paragraphs separate them.

Units. The lecture writes the speed of a computer in flops, floating-point operations per second, and lists the prefixes: teraflops \(10^{12}\), petaflops \(10^{15}\), exaflops \(10^{18}\), zettaflops \(10^{21}\), yottaflops \(10^{24}\). The slide spells the unit as FLOPS, flops and flop/s. This book writes flop for an operation and flop/s for a rate, because the lowercase plural flops is ambiguous between the two. A flop is one operation on two 64-bit operands, as defined in the model section; a rate is incomplete without its precision, and the Linpack rules below fix it at 64 bits.

The Linpack rule. The lecture names the TOP500 list and its benchmark, High Performance Linpack (HPL). The TOP500 project describes the benchmark as the solution of a dense system of linear equations. It lets the user scale the problem and optimize the software, and it reports four numbers: \(R_{\max}\), the highest rate achieved, at the problem size \(N_{\max}\); \(N_{1/2}\), the problem size at which half of \(R_{\max}\) is reached; and \(R_{\text{peak}}\), the theoretical peak. The rules require LU factorization with partial pivoting and an operation count of \(\tfrac23 n^3+O(n^2)\) double-precision operations for a system of order \(n\). They exclude fast matrix multiplication such as Strassen's method, solving in lower precision and refining iteratively, and emulating floating-point arithmetic in integers (TOP500, Linpack project page). The reference implementation, HPL, solves a random dense system in 64-bit arithmetic on distributed-memory computers with a two-dimensional block-cyclic data distribution and requires an MPI library and a BLAS library (netlib, HPL 2.3). Because the operation count is fixed by the rules, the reported rate is the fixed count divided by the measured time, and \(R_{\max}\) is a property of a machine running one program, not a peak.

The efficiency \(R_{\max}/R_{\text{peak}}\) of Frontier in June 2024 is 0.703: the Linpack program, which is the best case for regular arithmetic, reached 70 % of the theoretical peak. The average theoretical peak per counted core is 197 Gflop/s. That is 2.8 times the 70.4 Gflop/s that equation (6.1) gives for one P-core of the laptop at 4.4 GHz (computed in the section on the node), so a "core" in the TOP500 count is not a CPU core of the kind measured in this chapter; the lecture's slide says the count combines CPU and GPU cores, and an average over both kinds cannot be compared with the rate of a CPU core. The energy figure, 52.9 Gflop/s per watt on the Linpack run, is the ratio of \(R_{\max}\) to the power the list reports. The June 2026 problem has \(N_{\max}=24{,}837{,}120\); its operation count \(\tfrac23N_{\max}^3\) is \(1.02\times10^{22}\) flop, which at \(R_{\max}\) takes 7549 s, 2.10 hours, and its matrix of \(8N_{\max}^2=4.9\times10^{15}\) bytes shows that the benchmark is sized by memory capacity. The operation count grows as \(n^3\) and the data as \(n^2\), so the work per byte grows linearly with \(n\); that is why this benchmark approaches the peak rate where a stencil sweep, which does a constant number of operations per byte, does not (chapter 10).

A moving target. The lecture defines a supercomputer as a system whose performance is close to the highest rate of the day, so that yesterday's supercomputers stop being supercomputers. The lists give this a number. In June 2008 rank 1 (Roadrunner) had \(R_{\max}=1{,}026\) Tflop/s and rank 2 had 478.2 Tflop/s, so one system was above 1 Pflop/s. Rank 500 had 9.00 Tflop/s in June 2008 and 1.02 Pflop/s in June 2019 (June 2008, June 2019, page 5): the level of the unique leader of June 2008 was reached by rank 500 eleven years later. The doubling times over 18 years, from the listing, are 1.63 years for rank 1 and 2.19 years for rank 500. The lecture's statement that Frontier is the most powerful system has a date: Frontier was rank 1 from June 2022 to June 2024 and rank 2 from November 2024 (TOP500, Frontier); in the June 2026 list, rank 1 is LineShine with 2,198.40 Pflop/s and Frontier is rank 3 with 1,353.00 Pflop/s (June 2026). The listing gives the ratio of the two \(R_{\max}\) values, 1.62.

Computation as a third mode of science, and the definition of HPC. The lecture describes the traditional route of science as theory followed by experiment, with Newton's gravitation and Galileo's remark that mathematics is the alphabet of the universe, and adds computation as the third mode: computational science combines a science, its mathematics and computer science. It defines high performance computing as a set of computing technology derived from standard technology for fast numerical simulation, modeling and large data processing, built on parallel processing, and aimed at "grand challenge" problems that need much mathematical computation. It adds a warning: an arbitrary desktop application does not run fast on a supercomputer, because only applications that expose parallel algorithms do. The section on parallel computing turns that warning into a computable property. The lecture contrasts parallel computing, with processing elements closely coupled, and distributed computing, with isolated ones, and gives 50 machines on a gigabit Ethernet network in a laboratory as an example of the second. The two share the principle that a large problem is divided into small problems that are solved concurrently; the difference is the cost of communication, which chapter 14 models with \(T_{\text{msg}}(m)=\alpha+\beta m\).

The applications that the lecture lists are weather, climate and air quality; genome sequencing and the design of drugs and vaccines; materials design from electronic structure; astrophysical imaging; crash simulation instead of crashing prototypes; aerodynamic design with computational fluid dynamics; and the structural analysis of buildings with the finite element method. The share of supercomputing capacity given to COVID-19 research is the lecturer's statement; no source was sought.

The components of a cluster and the path of a job W2L3 18:00

The lecture presents the cluster as the popular architecture of supercomputers and names its parts on one diagram. The parts are of five kinds. Compute nodes run the parallel program; the lecture calls the collection of them the source of the compute power. Accelerated compute nodes are nodes that also carry accelerators, graphics processing units that the lecture says use the single-instruction-multiple-data (SIMD) principle, defined in the section on Flynn's taxonomy. The interconnect joins the nodes: the diagram shows a switch fabric and a "primary interconnect" that the lecture says gives high bandwidth and low latency, with ParamNet and InfiniBand as examples, and a separate gigabit Ethernet (1 GbE) network for administration. The storage has three layers in the diagram: a parallel file system of many rotating disks, a "storage acceleration" layer of solid-state drives that the lecture says holds frequently used code and data, and a hierarchical storage manager with a backup server and a tape library for backup and restore. The access and management nodes are the login nodes behind a networking gateway with a firewall, through which users reach the cluster from a local or remote network, and the boot servers and management nodes, which boot the compute nodes over the network and let the administrator manage every element.

The formal model of this chapter is a partition of the nodes of a cluster by role, with one rule of access. Let \(\mathcal N\) be the set of nodes and \(\rho:\mathcal N\to\{\text{login},\text{compute},\text{storage},\text{management}\}\) a role assignment, where a compute node may also carry an accelerator. Users connect only to nodes with \(\rho=\text{login}\); a program runs only on nodes with \(\rho=\text{compute}\) that a scheduler has allocated to it; every node reaches the storage nodes through the interconnect. The lecture describes the path of a job in these terms: the user logs in with credentials on a login node, submits a job, the job runs on free compute nodes, and the job completes. In steps:

  1. The user connects to a login node through the gateway and obtains a shell. The lecture says that the cluster uses Linux as its operating system and that the login node is where the user gets the prompt.
  2. The user submits a job description (the resources wanted and the command to run) to the scheduler. The lecture names SLURM.
  3. The scheduler allocates compute nodes. The Slurm overview states its three functions: it allocates exclusive or non-exclusive access to resources (compute nodes) to users for some time, it provides a framework for starting, executing and monitoring work, normally a parallel job, on the allocated nodes, and it arbitrates contention for resources by managing a queue of pending work (Slurm Workload Manager, Overview, retrieved 2026-10-26).
  4. The program reads its input and code from the home area and writes scratch data to the parallel file system; the lecture assigns the home area to NFS and the scratch area to Lustre.
  5. When the job ends, the nodes return to the pool, and the output remains on the file systems.

The lecture's software stack, drawn as three bands, has the same structure. The bottom band is the operating system, Linux in a stripped-down form, with CentOS 7.x on the slide and Debian or Fedora named as alternatives. The middle band is "middleware, applications and management": resource management and job scheduling (SLURM), the file systems (NFS, local file systems XFS and EXT4, Lustre), provisioning (OpenHPC with Warewulf) and cluster monitoring (Ganglia, Nagios, XDMoD). The lecture explains the arrows of its slide as a chain: the user submits to SLURM; SLURM uses the provisioning tools to assign free nodes; the provisioning tools use the monitoring tools to learn which nodes are free. The top band is the programming tools: application libraries (MPI implementations, MVAPICH2, Open MPI and Intel MPI, and visualization tools), development tools (Intel Cluster Studio or the GNU compilers) and performance monitoring (HPCC, IMB, IOR, FMGUI). OpenHPC describes itself as a collaborative community effort that aggregates the common ingredients for deploying and managing HPC Linux clusters, and lists provisioning tools, resource management, I/O clients, development tools and scientific libraries (OpenHPC, retrieved 2026-10-26). The slide's version numbers (CentOS 7.x) date the stack; a cluster built today uses newer releases of the same roles.

What a parallel file system does. The lecture says that a parallel file system lets many processes on the compute nodes operate on one file, each on a part of it. The Lustre manual gives the mechanism. A Lustre file system has metadata servers (MDS) with metadata targets (MDT) that hold the names and the layout of files, object storage servers (OSS) with object storage targets (OST) that hold the file data, and clients, the compute nodes (§1.2.2). A client first asks the MDT for the layout of a file, then reads and writes the objects directly on the OSS nodes, and the manual states that several clients can write to different parts of the same file simultaneously (§1.2.2), which is the lecture's statement. A file can be striped: its data is cut into chunks of stripe_size bytes and stored round-robin in stripe_count objects on different OSTs, a RAID-0 pattern. The default is a stripe count of 1 and a stripe size of 1 MB, and the user can change both per file or per directory (§1.3.1). The manual states the bandwidth rule in §1.3: the network bandwidth is the aggregate bandwidth of the OSS nodes to the targets; the disk bandwidth is the sum of the bandwidths of the storage targets up to the limit of the network bandwidth; and the aggregate bandwidth is the minimum of the two (Lustre Software Release 2.x Operations Manual, retrieved 2026-10-26). Let \(k\) be the stripe count, \(b_{\text{ost}}\) the bandwidth of one target and \(B_{\text{link}}\) the bandwidth of the link between a client and the servers. For one file written by one client, the rule gives

\[ B(k)=\min\bigl(k\,b_{\text{ost}},\;B_{\text{link}}\bigr). \tag{6.2} \]

The manual states the rule for a file system; the form (6.2) for one file is this chapter's reading of it, and it assumes that the client's data are spread evenly over the objects. The program below does two things. It computes, for a file of 1000 MiB plus 123 bytes, how many bytes each object receives under round-robin striping with 1 MiB chunks, and checks that the shares add up to the file size. Then it evaluates (6.2) with assumed values, 2 GB/s per target and 12 GB/s for the link; these are model parameters, not measurements of any system. The output is deterministic and is checked exactly.

"""Round-robin striping of one file over objects, and the bandwidth rule of a parallel file system.
The bandwidths are assumed model values, not measurements."""
MiB = 2**20


def bytes_per_object(file_bytes, stripe_size, stripe_count):
    """Chunk j of the file (bytes j*stripe_size onward) is stored in object j mod stripe_count."""
    got = [0] * stripe_count
    for j in range((file_bytes + stripe_size - 1) // stripe_size):
        got[j % stripe_count] += min(stripe_size, file_bytes - j * stripe_size)
    return got


def write_seconds(file_bytes, stripe_count, ost_gbps, link_gbps):
    """Each object is written at ost_gbps, in parallel; the client link caps the total at link_gbps."""
    slowest_object = max(bytes_per_object(file_bytes, MiB, stripe_count)) / (ost_gbps * 1e9)
    link_limit = file_bytes / (link_gbps * 1e9)
    return max(slowest_object, link_limit)


size = 1000 * MiB + 123                    # a file whose size is not a multiple of the stripe size
for k in (1, 3, 8):
    got = bytes_per_object(size, MiB, k)
    assert sum(got) == size
    print(f"stripe_count {k}: first object {got[0]} B, last object {got[-1]} B, "
          f"largest share {max(got) / size:.3f} of the file")

ost, link = 2.0, 12.0                      # assumed: 2 GB/s per object storage target, 12 GB/s client link
print("stripe_count  seconds  delivered GB/s  min(k*ost, link)")
for k in (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 16):
    t = write_seconds(size, k, ost, link)
    print(f"{k:12d} {t:8.3f} {size / t / 1e9:15.2f} {min(k * ost, link):12.1f}")

With the default stripe count of 1, the whole file sits in one object and the delivered rate is that of a single target, 2.0 GB/s, however many targets the file system has. The rate rises linearly with \(k\) until \(k\,b_{\text{ost}}\) reaches the link rate, at \(k=B_{\text{link}}/b_{\text{ost}}=6\), where the delivered rate is 11.98 GB/s, and a stripe count above 6 buys nothing in the model. The 11.98 instead of 12.00 at \(k=6\) comes from the chunk count: 1000 chunks do not divide evenly over 6 objects, so four objects hold 167 chunks and two hold 166. The largest object holds 0.167 of the file, and its write time at 2 GB/s, 0.0876 s, is slightly longer than the link time, 0.0874 s. The 123 extra bytes do not change this result. Striping a file is therefore a decision with a numerical consequence, and the right stripe count depends on how many clients write the file and how fast each client's link is. The model contains no cost for the metadata work or for small files, so it cannot say when a stripe count above 1 hurts; the manual has a section on striping considerations (§19.2) that this chapter did not read.

The interconnect enters the cost of every message. The book writes the time to send \(m\) bytes between two nodes as \(T_{\text{msg}}(m)=\alpha+\beta m\), where \(\alpha\) is the latency in seconds and \(\beta\) the inverse bandwidth in seconds per byte; chapter 14 measures both on this machine and fits them. The lecture's requirement of "high bandwidth and low latency" is the requirement of small \(\beta\) and small \(\alpha\).

Why parallel: capacity, time, and the concurrency a program offers W2L3 25:51

The lecture gives two reasons for parallel computing and a third that combines them. A single CPU is slow, so a program that runs on it may take days or weeks; with more CPUs the job divides into smaller jobs that finish sooner. A single node has limited memory, so the data may not fit; with more CPUs, each with its own memory, the total memory grows. A user can therefore save time, solve a larger problem, or solve a larger problem in a shorter time.

The capacity argument, in numbers. The book's Jacobi problem stores two arrays \(u\) and \(u_{\text{new}}\) of \((N+2)^2\) doubles, so the memory is \(16(N+2)^2\) bytes. On the 15 GiB of the guest the largest grid has \(N\approx\sqrt{15\cdot2^{30}/16}-2\approx31{,}700\) points per side, if nothing else uses memory. A grid of \(N=10^{6}\) needs \(16\times(10^6+2)^2=1.6\times10^{13}\) bytes, 16 TB, which is at least 994 nodes of 15 GiB. The memory per side grows as the square root of the number of nodes, \(N\propto\sqrt{n_{\text{node}}}\), so a thousandfold increase in memory buys a 32-fold increase in the number of points per side. Memory capacity decides whether a problem can run at all, and the computing time decides whether it finishes; the next paragraphs define the property that decides the second.

The route goes from an algorithm to a parallel algorithm, to code in a language such as C or Python, to a program compiled with a parallel framework such as OpenMP or MPI, and then to workflows or problem-solving environments with a graphical interface, through which domain experts use the program without seeing the hardware or the framework. Some such applications are commercial products; the lecture names MATLAB. The rest of this section concerns the second step, the parallel algorithm, and the property that decides whether it exists.

Concurrency as a property of the computation. The lecture states that a parallel algorithm must show concurrency, and that if the output of the first part is the input of the next, concurrency is lost and the program is unsuited to a supercomputer. The slide defines concurrency as the ability to perform many actions simultaneously. This definition can be made exact. Describe a computation as a directed acyclic graph whose nodes are operations and whose edges say that an operation needs the result of another. The work \(W\) is the number of operations, and the span \(D\) is the number of operations on the longest path, the length of the longest chain of dependences. The ratio \(W/D\) is the concurrency of the computation. The lecture's "output of the first is the input of the next" is a graph that is one path: \(D=W\) and the concurrency is 1. Two bounds follow, for \(p\) processing elements that each execute one operation per time step. First, no schedule can finish before \(D\) steps, because the longest chain is sequential, and none before \(W/p\) steps, because \(p\) elements do at most \(p\) operations per step; so \(T_p\ge\max(D,W/p)\) and

\[ S_p=\frac{T_1}{T_p}\le\min\Bigl(p,\frac{W}{D}\Bigr),\qquad T_p\le\frac{W-D}{p}+D\ \text{ for a greedy schedule.} \tag{6.4} \]

A greedy schedule never leaves a processing element idle while an operation is ready. The second bound is proved as follows. Call a step complete if \(p\) operations run in it, and incomplete otherwise. In an incomplete step every ready operation runs, so the length of the longest path in the remaining graph falls by one; there are therefore at most \(D\) incomplete steps, say \(I\le D\), each running at least one operation. The complete steps run \(p\) operations each, so their number \(C\) satisfies \(pC\le W-I\), and \(T_p=C+I\le (W-I)/p+I=W/p+I(1-1/p)\le W/p+D(1-1/p)=(W-D)/p+D\). Brent (1974) proved a bound of this kind for the evaluation of arithmetic expressions: his abstract states that an expression with \(n\) variables and constants, using addition, multiplication and division with any nesting, can be evaluated in time \(4\log_2 n+10(n-1)/p\) on \(p\) processors that each perform an operation in unit time, and that this is within a constant factor of the best possible (Journal of the ACM 21(2), 201–206; abstract only). The bound (6.4) is the general form that the proof above gives; the expression result is the special case that Brent's paper treats.

The program builds the graphs of three ways to add \(n=1024\) numbers and computes \(W\), \(D\), the concurrency, and the time of a greedy schedule for \(p=1,4,16,64\), together with the bound of (6.4). The serial chain adds term after term to a running total. The balanced tree adds pairs, then pairs of pairs. The third computation is what an OpenMP reduction does: 8 threads each form a private partial sum of 128 terms, and one thread then adds the 8 partial sums in a chain. The program checks \(T_1=W\) and that every simulated time is at most the bound. It is deterministic, and its output is checked exactly.

"""Work, span and greedy schedules of three computations that each add n = 1024 numbers."""
import heapq
from math import ceil


def chain(n):
    """Serial sum: operation i adds term i + 1 to the running total, so it needs operation i - 1."""
    return {i: ([i - 1] if i else []) for i in range(n - 1)}


def tree(n):
    """Balanced tree: the n inputs are values, not operations; each operation adds two values."""
    deps, level, nid = {}, [None] * n, 0
    while len(level) > 1:
        nxt = []
        for a, b in zip(level[0::2], level[1::2]):
            deps[nid] = [x for x in (a, b) if x is not None]
            nxt.append(nid)
            nid += 1
        level = nxt
    return deps


def partial_sums(n, p):
    """p private partial sums (chains of n/p - 1 adds), then a chain of p - 1 adds: an OpenMP reduction."""
    deps, ids, last = {}, 0, []
    for _ in range(p):
        prev = None
        for _ in range(n // p - 1):
            deps[ids] = [] if prev is None else [prev]
            prev = ids
            ids += 1
        last.append(prev)
    acc = last[0]
    for t in last[1:]:
        deps[ids] = [acc, t]
        acc = ids
        ids += 1
    return deps


def work_span(deps):
    """Work = number of operations; span = length of the longest dependence chain."""
    depth = {}
    for v in sorted(deps):                  # node ids are topologically ordered in all builders
        depth[v] = 1 + max((depth[u] for u in deps[v]), default=0)
    return len(deps), max(depth.values())


def greedy_time(deps, p):
    """Greedy schedule: at each step run up to p ready operations, one time unit each."""
    waiting = {v: len(us) for v, us in deps.items()}
    users = {v: [] for v in deps}
    for v, us in deps.items():
        for u in us:
            users[u].append(v)
    ready = [v for v, k in waiting.items() if k == 0]
    heapq.heapify(ready)
    steps = 0
    while ready:
        batch = [heapq.heappop(ready) for _ in range(min(p, len(ready)))]
        for v in batch:
            for w in users[v]:
                waiting[w] -= 1
                if waiting[w] == 0:
                    heapq.heappush(ready, w)
        steps += 1
    return steps

The hardware is a processor connected to a main memory that holds data and code, with an input device and an output device. The basic computing model then follows one statement down through the layers. A programmer writes a = a + b + 1 in a high-level language. The processor understands machine language, whose one-to-one text form is assembly language, and a compiler converts the statement to a series of instructions. The lecture's example is ADD A, B followed by INC A. Each instruction has an opcode, which names the operation (ADD, INC), and operands, which locate the data (here the registers A and B). The instructions and the data are stored in memory, in separate areas, and the result goes back to storage. The interface between the software that supplies the instructions and the hardware that executes them is the instruction set architecture (ISA).

Time per instruction: latency and throughput. The ISA says what an instruction computes. The time it takes depends on two numbers defined in the model section: the latency \(L\) of an instruction, the cycles from ready operands to the ready result, and the throughput \(X\), the instructions of that kind that the core can start per cycle. The two limit a program in different ways. A chain of instructions in which each needs the result of the previous one (a dependence chain) advances by one link per \(L\) cycles, however many instructions the core could start. A set of \(K\) chains that depend on each other not at all advances by \(K\) links per \(L\) cycles, as long as \(K/L\le X\). The rate in instructions per cycle is therefore

\[ R(K)=\min\Bigl(\frac{K}{L},\,X\Bigr),\qquad K^{*}=L\,X, \tag{6.5} \]

and \(K^{*}\) is the smallest number of independent chains that reaches the throughput limit. This is Little's law applied to the core. Little (1961) shows, for a queue with a stationary process, that the mean number of units in the system equals the mean arrival rate times the mean time a unit spends in the system (abstract of Operations Research 9(3), 383–387; abstract only). The units are the instructions in flight, the time in the system is \(L\), and the rate is \(X\): to complete \(X\) instructions per cycle, \(L X\) instructions must be in flight, and a chain contributes at most one. For the P-core, Intel's manual gives \(X=2\) for 256-bit multiply and FMA (Table 2-4, p. 2-14) and a latency of 4 cycles for FMA (Table 2-5, "FMA/0,1/4": execution unit, ports 0 and 1, latency 4), so \(K^{*}=8\). A scalar FMA is 2 flops, so (6.5) predicts \(2R(K)\) flop per cycle: 0.5, 1, 2, 4 and 4 for \(K=1,2,4,8,12\).

Flynn's taxonomy, vector lanes, and the node as a multiple-instruction machine W2L4 6:55

The lecture credits the classification of architectures by streams of instructions and streams of data to Michael J. Flynn, 1966. The first paper that did so is "Very high-speed computing systems", Proceedings of the IEEE 54(12), December 1966, pp. 1901–1909. Its abstract, which is the only part of it that this chapter read, states the four classes and defines the term: "Stream", as used there, refers to "the sequence of data or instructions as seen by the machine during the execution of a program" (Flynn 1966, abstract; bibliographic data checked in Crossref, abstract text from OpenAlex). The lecture's four classes and its slides are these.

  • Single instruction, single data (SISD). One instruction stream drives one execution unit that reads one data stream. The lecture says this is the sequential computer and the building block of parallel systems.
  • Single instruction, multiple data (SIMD). One instruction stream drives several execution units, each reading its own part of the data. The slide says the first use was in vector supercomputers such as the Cray platforms, that SIMD instructions are executed by the ALU of modern processors, that the model suits streaming data such as audio and video, and that GPUs use it.
  • Multiple instruction, single data (MISD). Several instruction streams act on one data stream. The lecture calls it rarely implemented and lists fault tolerance, multiple frequency filters and the cracking of messages coded with cryptographic techniques as possible uses.
  • Multiple instruction, multiple data (MIMD). Independent processors execute different instructions on their own data. The lecture says most parallel computers are of this type, with two implementations, shared memory and distributed memory.

A clarification. In SIMD every execution unit receives the same instruction. The slide shows the instruction I1 going to both execution units, and the slide for MISD is the one that shows I1 and I2.

A formal reading. Let a stream be as in the quotation, and let \(m_I\) and \(m_D\) be the numbers of instruction streams and of data streams that the machine processes at one time, at a stated level of description. The class is the pair of answers to "is \(m_I\) greater than 1?" and "is \(m_D\) greater than 1?". The level matters, and the same machine belongs to different classes at different levels, as the table shows for this chapter's laptop.

Flynn's 1972 paper, "Some computer organizations and their effectiveness", IEEE Transactions on Computers C-21(9), 948–960, analyzes the classes by their limits (Flynn 1972, abstract only). Its abstract states that the overlapped simplex processor (SISD) is limited by data dependencies and that branching has a particularly degenerative effect, which is the dependence-chain limit of (6.5) seen from the architecture side; that for SIMD a "nesting type" explanation is offered for Minsky's conjecture that performance grows as \(\log M\) rather than \(M\) with \(M\) data-stream processors; and that multiprocessors (MIMD) saturate when the fraction of task time spent locked out approaches \(1/n\) for \(n\) processors. This chapter uses only the first of the three.

SIMD in one core. A 256-bit vector register holds \(\ell=4\) doubles, and one vector FMA performs 4 multiply-adds, 8 flops, with the same latency and, in Intel's table, the same throughput as the scalar instruction (Table 2-4 gives "2x256-bit" for the vector unit). Then (6.5) holds for vector chains with the same \(K^{*}=8\) and the flop rate is multiplied by \(\ell\): \(\phi=2X\ell=16\) flop per cycle. /* Clock estimate: 16 dependent integer adds per trip, one cycle each; the add reads a
   register, because an add of an immediate can be executed faster on this CPU. */
static double clock_ghz(void)
{
    const long n = 20000000L;
    long x = 0, y = 1;
    double t0 = now();
    for (long i = 0; i < n; i++) {
#define A asm volatile("add %1, %0" : "+r"(x) : "r"(y));
        A A A A A A A A A A A A A A A A
    }
    return 16.0 * n / (now() - t0) * 1e-9 + (x < 0);
}

The same loop, five builds. A compiler decides whether a loop uses these instructions. The program below writes the polynomial loop of the running case to a file, builds it with five sets of gcc flags, counts the packed floating-point instructions in each binary (packed multiply, packed add, and packed FMA), times it, and prints a checksum of the output. The data are 2048 doubles in and 2048 out, 32 KiB, so the loop runs from the L1 cache. The flag -O1 and the flags -O2 -fno-tree-vectorize give scalar code. The flag -O2 alone already gives vector code in gcc 13; the packed instructions in that binary are the SSE forms mulpd and addpd, which use 128-bit registers (2 doubles), and not the AVX forms. The node as a multiple-instruction machine. The next program runs the kernel of the previous experiments in \(p\) threads at once and prints a total rate in Gflop/s, for 1 and for 10 chains per thread. One chain per thread uses about \(1/8\) of the FMA capacity of a P-core and \(1/6\) of an E-core, by (6.5). Ten chains per thread saturate it. Each thread starts from different values and the sums are reduced at the end so that the compiler cannot remove the work. The number of threads is set with omp_set_num_threads; the threads are not pinned, so the operating system and the hypervisor place them. The parallel region ends at its implicit barrier and every thread does the same work \(W_1\), so the program prints \(p\) times the work of one thread divided by the time of the slowest thread:

\[ R_{\text{printed}}=\frac{p\,W_1}{\max_t T_t}=p\,r_{\min},\qquad r_{\min}=\frac{W_1}{\max_t T_t}, \tag{6.6} \]

where \(T_t\) is the time of thread \(t\) and \(r_{\min}\) the rate of the slowest thread. The printed rate is not the sum of the rates of the threads; it equals that sum only if all threads run at the same rate.

#include <stdio.h>
#include <omp.h>
#include <immintrin.h>

/* Register-resident 256-bit FMA kernel.  chains = 1: one dependent chain per thread (uses about
   1/8 of a P-core's FMA capacity).  chains = 10: ten independent chains (saturates the FMA units).
   Chains start from different values so the compiler cannot merge them. */
static double kernel(long n, int chains)
{
    __m256d b = _mm256_set1_pd(0.9999999), c = _mm256_set1_pd(1e-9);
    __m256d a0 = _mm256_set1_pd(1), a1 = _mm256_set1_pd(2), a2 = _mm256_set1_pd(3), a3 = _mm256_set1_pd(4),
            a4 = _mm256_set1_pd(5), a5 = _mm256_set1_pd(6), a6 = _mm256_set1_pd(7), a7 = _mm256_set1_pd(8),
            a8 = _mm256_set1_pd(9), a9 = _mm256_set1_pd(10);
    if (chains == 1) {
        for (long i = 0; i < n; i++) a0 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a0, b, c);
    } else {
        for (long i = 0; i < n; i++) {
            a0 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a0, b, c); a1 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a1, b, c); a2 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a2, b, c);
            a3 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a3, b, c); a4 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a4, b, c); a5 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a5, b, c);
            a6 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a6, b, c); a7 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a7, b, c); a8 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a8, b, c);
            a9 = _mm256_fmadd_pd(a9, b, c);
        }
    }
    __m256d s = _mm256_add_pd(_mm256_add_pd(_mm256_add_pd(a0, a1), _mm256_add_pd(a2, a3)),
                              _mm256_add_pd(_mm256_add_pd(a4, a5), _mm256_add_pd(_mm256_add_pd(a6, a7), _mm256_add_pd(a8, a9))));
    double t[4];
    _mm256_storeu_pd(t, s);
    return t[0] + t[1] + t[2] + t[3];
}

/* Total Gflop/s of p threads that each run the kernel; best of 3 runs. */
static double total_gflops(int p, int chains, long n)
{
    double best = 1e30, sink = 0.0;
    omp_set_num_threads(p);
    for (int r = 0; r < 3; r++) {
        double t0 = omp_get_wtime();
        #pragma omp parallel reduction(+:sink)
        sink += kernel(n, chains);
        double t = omp_get_wtime() - t0;
        if (t < best) best = t;
    }
    return 8.0 * chains * n * p / best * 1e-9 + (sink < 0);
}

int main(void)
{
    int ps[] = {1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12};
    printf("%3s %22s %22s\n", "p", "1 chain/thread Gflop/s", "10 chains/thread Gflop/s");
    for (int j = 0; j < 6; j++)
        printf("%3d %22.1f %22.1f\n", ps[j], total_gflops(ps[j], 1, 250000000L), total_gflops(ps[j], 10, 25000000L));
    return 0;
}

Programs, processes, and sharing one core: multiprogramming, time slicing, the context switch W2L4 12:27

The lecture connects the architectures to the programs that run on them through the interface of the instruction set. A program is a sequence of instructions, and the processor executes it instruction by instruction. The slide on process basics shows the route from source code to execution: the compiler turns source code into object code, the linker turns object code into binary code, the binary is stored on disk as a program file, and the loader places it in main memory. The lecture defines a process as the program that has been loaded and runs. While it runs, it needs resources: CPU time, more memory when it calls an allocation routine such as malloc, and files on disk. It asks the operating system for them and the operating system manages them for the life of the process. This chapter takes the definition one step further: a process is a program in execution with its own address space, so that two processes do not see each other's data unless the operating system arranges it.

Single tasking. The lecture's first example is the original IBM PC: an Intel 8088 at 4.77 MHz, 256 KB of memory, MS-DOS. The slide states that early systems allowed one program at a time and that the running process had complete control of the system and all its resources. The timeline on the slide is the sequence operating system, loader, process P1 (with a system call in the middle), operating system, loader, process P2. The processor is idle whenever the program waits, for example for input from the user or a block from the disk.

Multiprogramming. The lecture describes multiprogramming as the use of that idle time: several processes are in memory, CPU scheduling is its basic characteristic, and when the running process waits for an input or output request, the operating system takes the CPU from it and gives it to another process, so that some process is always running. The lecture also mentions terminate-and-stay-resident programs of MS-DOS, which stay in memory and are invoked at intervals. A simple model shows how much this can gain. Let a process spend a fraction \(w\) of its time waiting for input and output, and suppose that \(n\) processes are in memory and that their waits are independent. The CPU is idle only when all \(n\) wait at once, which has probability \(w^n\), so the CPU utilization is

\[ U(n)=1-w^{\,n}. \tag{6.7} \]

For \(w=0.8\), which is a program that computes during 20 % of its time, \(U(1)=0.20\), \(U(4)=1-0.4096=0.59\) and \(U(10)=1-0.1074=0.89\); the slide on scheduling criteria states that in real systems the CPU utilization typically varies between 40 % and 90 %, a claim without a source on the slide. The model assumes independence of the waits, ignores the cost of switching and ignores the limit that memory puts on \(n\); it is a derivation under these assumptions, not a measurement. It is the reason for the lecture's statement that the objective of multiprogramming is to keep the CPU as busy as possible. It also shows what multiprogramming does not do: it raises the utilization of one CPU and does not shorten any process, and a process that never waits (a long calculation) gains nothing from it.

Multitasking by time slices. The lecture's multitasking is the second way to share one CPU. Each process receives a slice of time, milliseconds or microseconds; when the slice ends, control returns to the operating system, which gives the CPU to another process. The slide states that this needs hardware support inside the CPU, which was not present in the 8088 and is present in the 80286 and later processors from Intel, and that the operating system also switches when the running process waits for an input or output operation or an event. The lecture adds that the effect is an impression that all processes run continuously. The lecture's diagram of two processes (its source line reads "OS Principles - Galvi…", cut off in the frame) shows the mechanism. An interrupt or a system call transfers control to the operating system. The operating system saves the state of process P1 in the process control block of P1 (PCB1) and reloads the state saved earlier in PCB2, and process P2 runs; at its next interrupt or system call the same exchange happens in the other direction. This exchange is the context switch. The state that it saves and restores is the registers, the program counter and the description of the address space and open files; the lecture adds that the hardware (a timer, and dedicated support) takes part.

The hardware claim, checked against a data sheet. The Intel data sheet of the M80C286, a low-power version of the 80286 that the data sheet calls compatible with the HMOS M80286, describes the processor as designed for multi-user and multi-tasking applications. It states that the processor has built-in memory protection with a four-level protection mechanism for task isolation, a hardware task switching facility, and memory management that maps \(2^{30}\) bytes of virtual address space per task into \(2^{24}\) bytes of physical memory, and that one instruction can end the execution of one task, save its state, switch to a new task, load its state and start it (Intel M80C286 data sheet, introduction, p. 1 of the file). This supports the lecture's statement that the 80286 has hardware support for multitasking that the 8088 lacks, in the sense of isolation between tasks and a one-instruction task switch. It does not settle a narrower question: whether time slicing as such is impossible on a processor without these features. Time slicing needs at least a periodic interrupt that returns control to the operating system, and this chapter did not open a document that says whether the 8088 system of the lecture had one. The lecture's statement is therefore supported for protection and task switching and open for time slicing alone.

The cost of a switch, measured. A context switch costs time during which no program computes. The program below measures an upper bound on it. Two processes (or, in the other mode, two threads) are connected by two pipes and pinned to one logical processor with sched_setaffinity; the parent writes one byte, the other side reads it and writes it back, and the parent reads the echo. Each round trip makes the single processor run first one side and then the other, so it contains two context switches and four system calls (two writes, two reads). The program prints the time per round trip and half of it. The measured quantity is the latency of a one-byte pipe exchange between two schedulable entities that share one logical processor. It is not the time of the context switch alone, because the system calls are inside it. The listing runs both modes twice. The output is a sample, and the check is the return code.

#define _GNU_SOURCE
#include <pthread.h>
#include <sched.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <string.h>
#include <sys/wait.h>
#include <time.h>
#include <unistd.h>

#define ROUNDS 200000

static int ab[2], ba[2];                    /* pipe A->B and pipe B->A */

static double now(void)
{
    struct timespec ts;
    clock_gettime(CLOCK_MONOTONIC, &ts);
    return ts.tv_sec + 1e-9 * ts.tv_nsec;
}

static void pin(int cpu)
{
    cpu_set_t set;
    CPU_ZERO(&set);
    CPU_SET(cpu, &set);
    if (sched_setaffinity(0, sizeof set, &set)) { perror("sched_setaffinity"); exit(1); }
}

static void echo_side(void)                 /* reads one byte, writes it back, ROUNDS times */
{
    char c;
    for (int i = 0; i < ROUNDS; i++) {
        if (read(ab[0], &c, 1) != 1 || write(ba[1], &c, 1) != 1) exit(1);
    }
}

static void *thread_main(void *arg) { (void)arg; echo_side(); return NULL; }

static double drive(void)                   /* the other side: write, then wait for the echo */
{
    char c = 'x';
    double t0 = now();
    for (int i = 0; i < ROUNDS; i++) {
        if (write(ab[1], &c, 1) != 1 || read(ba[0], &c, 1) != 1) exit(1);
    }
    return (now() - t0) / ROUNDS;
}

int main(int argc, char **argv)
{
    int threads = argc > 1 && strcmp(argv[1], "thread") == 0;
    int cpu = argc > 2 ? atoi(argv[2]) : 2;
    if (pipe(ab) || pipe(ba)) return 1;
    pin(cpu);                               /* children and new threads inherit this CPU mask */
    double rt;
    if (threads) {
        pthread_t t;
        pthread_create(&t, NULL, thread_main, NULL);
        rt = drive();
        pthread_join(t, NULL);
    } else {
        pid_t pid = fork();
        if (pid == 0) { echo_side(); _exit(0); }
        rt = drive();
        waitpid(pid, NULL, 0);
    }
    printf("%-7s on cpu %d: %.2f us per round trip, %.2f us per half round trip\n", threads ? "threads" : "process", cpu, rt * 1e6, rt * 1e6 / 2);
    return 0;
}

At the 58.7 Gflop/s of the vectorized polynomial loop, 1.3 to 2.2 µs is 76,000 to 130,000 flops, so one switch costs about \(10^5\) flops of lost work. If a time slice is \(q\) and a switch costs \(t_{cs}\), the share of the time that a core spends on switching is at most \(t_{cs}/(q+t_{cs})\); for \(t_{cs}=2\) µs and \(q=1\) ms this is 0.2 %. The cost is small for one process per core and large when many runnable threads compete for few cores, because each switch also disturbs the caches, which this measurement does not include.

The lecture says that switching between threads is quicker than switching between processes. This test does not show it. The ratio of the thread round trip to the process round trip was 1.03 and 0.90 in this sample and between 0.84 and 1.06 over the nine pairs of the four sessions, so the difference between the two modes is within the variation between runs. A switch between processes changes the address space and a switch between threads of one process does not, and the lecture's claim has that mechanism behind it. The hardware here may hide it (the guest's /proc/cpuinfo lists the pcid flag, shown in the session of the model section, which lets a processor keep address-space translations across a switch, but this chapter did not test whether it matters), and the machine is a virtual machine, so the test does not decide the claim. It shows only that on this machine, for a pipe exchange, the difference is below the noise of about 10 %.

Several processors. The lecture's slide on multiprocessor scheduling states that with more than one processor the load of processes and threads can be shared, but scheduling becomes more complex. In asymmetric multiprocessing a master takes the scheduling decisions; in symmetric multiprocessing each processor schedules itself and picks tasks from a ready queue. The diagram on the slide shows four processing elements, each with a local memory and a communication interface, joined by a communication network; it is the picture of the distributed-memory machine of the previous section, whereas the two definitions on the slide concern only who decides which thread runs where.

Threads and Hyper-Threading W2L4 25:26

The lecture defines a thread as a lightweight process and the basic unit of CPU utilization. A traditional process has one thread and a modern process may have several. The slide's table separates what is unique to a thread (a thread identifier, a program counter, a register set and a stack) from what a thread shares with the other threads of the same process (the code section, the data section, and operating-system resources such as open files). The Linux manual page pthreads(7) states the same division for POSIX threads: a process can contain multiple threads, all executing the same program, which share the same global memory (the data and heap segments), but each thread has its own stack of automatic variables (man7.org, pthreads(7)). The lecture's two reasons for threads are that a process with several threads can perform several tasks, and that one application often performs many similar tasks, as a web server does when each request from a browser is handled by its own thread. The slide lists four benefits: responsiveness, because one blocked thread does not block the others; sharing of resources, because the code, data and open files are common; economy, because switching threads is quicker; and suitability for multiprocessor architectures. It lists four criteria for scheduling: CPU utilization, throughput (more output in a given time), turnaround time and waiting time.

The division of storage in the table is the model that OpenMP uses in chapter 11. A variable that lives in the data section or on the heap is visible to every thread of the team, and a variable that lives on a thread's stack is that thread's own. Chapter 12 treats the clauses that change this. The multiple threads of an OpenMP program are the threads of this section, and their number and placement on the 12 logical processors of the laptop are decided by the operating system, as the lecture says.

Hyper-Threading. The lecture's slide on Intel's technology states that it allows more than one thread to run on each core, that the CPU exposes two execution contexts per physical core, that one physical core behaves like two logical cores that can handle different threads, that the technology duplicates the architectural state (5 % more die area) and shares one set of execution resources, that there is no loss if a single thread runs, that two logical cores can run tasks more efficiently than a single-threaded core by overcoming idle time while one task waits, that Intel claims up to 30 % more CPU throughput in server applications, that it is enabled through a BIOS setting, and, in red, that it is recommended to be disabled for HPC workloads. The slide gives the recommendation without a reason.

The source of the slide's numbers is the paper that described the first implementation, in the Intel Xeon processor MP. The abstract states that the technology makes a single physical processor appear as two logical processors, that the physical execution resources are shared and the architecture state is duplicated, and that the instructions of both logical processors "will persist and execute simultaneously on shared execution resources". The paper states that the die area cost of the first implementation was less than 5 % of the total (p. 4 of the paper), that each logical processor maintains a complete set of the architecture state (registers, control registers, interrupt-controller registers, some machine-state registers; p. 4), and, in the conclusion, that measured performance on the Xeon MP shows gains of up to 30 % "on common server application benchmarks" (Marr et al., Intel Technology Journal 6(1), 2002, abstract, p. 4 and p. 11 of the paper). Two limits on the slide's figures follow from the paper. The figure of 5 % is an upper bound ("less than 5 %") for that implementation. The figure of 30 % is an upper bound on a set of server benchmarks on a 2002 processor, and it is not a property of the technique on any processor. The mechanism, as the paper states it, is simultaneous: the two logical processors have instructions in flight at the same time and share the units, which is what the slide's "shares one set of execution resources" says; it is not a time slicing of one execution unit between two threads. Intel's current manual states that the P-cores of the 12th generation processors can have Hyper-Threading enabled (§2.3.1, p. 2-6). With 8 cores and 12 logical processors, as Windows reports, the 4 extra logical processors correspond to the 4 P-cores; that the E-cores have one thread each is this chapter's inference from the count.

A model that predicts the gain. The core model of equation (6.5) says how much of the FMA capacity of a core one thread uses. A thread with \(K\) independent chains issues \(K/L\) FMA per cycle, a fraction \(u=K/(LX)\) of the throughput \(X\) (for \(K\le LX\)). Suppose two identical threads share the execution units of one core, and that each would use the fraction \(u\) alone. Together they ask for \(2u\), and the core can deliver at most 1, so the core's total rate relative to one thread is

\[ g=\frac{\min(1,2u)}{u}=\min\Bigl(2,\;\frac{L\,X}{K}\Bigr). \tag{6.8} \]

For the P-core with \(LX=8\), a thread with one chain (\(u=1/8\)) gains \(g=2\): the second thread doubles the core's rate. A thread with 4 chains also gains \(g=2\). A thread with 6 chains gains \(g=8/6=1.33\), which is the order of Intel's 30 %. A thread with 8 or more chains already saturates the units and gains \(g=1\), and each of the two threads then runs at half of its single-thread speed. The model leaves out cache sharing and the partitioning of other resources that the paper describes, and it applies to one resource, the FMA units. What it contains is the lecture's own mechanism, the filling of idle time, with the measure of idle time that (6.5) provides: the rate of a core with two threads is between 1 and 2 times its rate with one, and the factor is set by how many execution slots one thread leaves unused, not by the technology.

The model also gives a reading of the recommendation to disable Hyper-Threading for HPC. For threads that saturate the units (\(g=1\)) a core delivers the same rate with one thread or with two. Equation (6.6) gives the speedup of a region whose \(p\) threads have equal shares: \(S_p=p\,v_{\min}\), where \(v_{\min}\) is the speed of the slowest occupied logical processor relative to a thread alone on a P-core. On a homogeneous node of \(c\) cores, \(c\) pinned threads with Hyper-Threading off and \(2c\) threads with it on therefore give the same speedup: \(S=c\cdot1\) and \(S=2c\cdot\tfrac12\). The model shows a loss only when \(p=c\) threads run with Hyper-Threading on and two of them share a core while another core idles: then \(v_{\min}=\tfrac12\) and \(S=c/2\). That is a failure of placement, which pinning or disabling the second hardware threads prevents. On the hybrid laptop the E-cores set \(v_{\min}=26.4/70.4=0.375\) in both cases, so \(S_8=8\times0.375=3.0\) with 8 threads and \(S_{12}=4.5\) with 12. The model therefore reads the recommendation as protection against bad placement, not as a consequence of saturation.

Slurm gives the resource manager that control. Its job option --hint=nomultithread means "don't use extra threads with in-core multi-threading", and the same page notes that in-core multi-threading "may also improve performance" for communication-intensive applications (Slurm, multi-core support, retrieved 2026-10-26). A resource manager can therefore keep jobs off the second hardware threads without a BIOS setting. The thread-scaling run does not test this reading: its rate is set by the slowest thread (section on Flynn's taxonomy), so it cannot show the gain or the loss of a second thread on a P-core.

The models of this chapter establish five things. The peak rate of a core is the product \(f\cdot2X\ell\), and for the P-core the factors \(X=2\) and \(L=4\) agree with the manual and with the measurements (6.1, 6.5). A program reaches the peak only if it exposes \(K\ge LX\) independent chains and uses the widest instructions, as the polynomial loop shows. The concurrency \(W/D\) of a computation bounds its speedup on any number of processing elements, and a greedy schedule finishes in at most \((W-D)/p+D\) steps (6.4), which is less than twice the lower bound \(\max(D,W/p)\). The bandwidth of a file on a striped parallel file system is the smaller of the sum of its targets and the link (6.2). The rate of a core with two threads is between 1 and 2 times its rate with one (6.8), and the speedup of a region with equal shares is set by the slowest thread (6.6).

The models leave out most of the machine. Equation (6.5) treats one instruction type and ignores the front end, the load and store ports (exercise 1 adds them), branch prediction and memory. The peak bounds use the highest clocks, which cores that run together do not hold, and the E-core bound rests on a third-party measurement database (uops.info). The measurements are single sessions on one hybrid laptop under a virtual machine, so the ranges quoted are variation between runs and not confidence intervals. The cluster section describes roles and a software stack and does not model queueing, and the striping model has assumed bandwidths. The multiprogramming model (6.7) assumes independent waits. The Jacobi problem of the book enters only as a memory-capacity calculation; its flop rate against its memory traffic is the subject of chapter 10.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive). The dot product \(s\leftarrow s+x_iy_i\) over arrays in the L1 cache needs two loads per FMA. Intel's Figure 2-2 labels the load path of the P-core "3x256", which this exercise reads as 3 loads of 256 bits per cycle. (a) Derive the throughput limit \(X_{\text{eff}}\) in FMA per cycle for 256-bit vectors, with FMA latency \(L=4\) and \(X=2\). (b) Derive the smallest number of independent accumulators \(K^{*}\) that reaches it. (c) At 4.0 GHz, predict the Gflop/s of the scalar and of the 256-bit version for \(K=1,2,4,8\).

Solution

(a) The core can start at most 2 FMA per cycle, and 3 loads per cycle supply \(3/2=1.5\) FMA per cycle, since each FMA consumes two loaded operands. The limit is the smaller: \(X_{\text{eff}}=\min(2,1.5)=1.5\) FMA per cycle. (b) By (6.5), \(K^{*}=LX_{\text{eff}}=4\times1.5=6\) accumulators, not 8: the loads, not the FMA units, saturate first, and a seventh and eighth accumulator add nothing. (c) The rate in FMA per cycle is \(R(K)=\min(K/4,1.5)\): 0.25, 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 for \(K=1,2,4,8\). A scalar FMA is 2 flops, so at 4.0 GHz the rates are \(2\cdot R\cdot4.0=2.0,\ 4.0,\ 8.0,\ 12.0\) Gflop/s; a 256-bit FMA is 8 flops, so the rates are 8.0, 16.0, 32.0 and 48.0 Gflop/s. The plateau of the vector dot product, 48 Gflop/s, is 75 % of the 64 Gflop/s of the pure FMA kernel at the same clock, because loads limit it. The derivation assumes that the data come from the L1 cache and that the "3x256" label means 3 loads of 256 bits per cycle; the label is the only source used for it, and a program from main memory is limited by bandwidth instead (chapter 10). At \(K=1\) the loads do not limit, so the prediction equals that of the pure FMA kernel: 2.0 and 8.0 Gflop/s at 4.0 GHz, which is 1.85 and 7.4 at the estimated 3.7 GHz, against the measured 1.87 and 7.50 of fma_scalar.c and fma_vector.c.

Exercise 2 (compute). A cluster has 100 nodes. Each node has 2 sockets, each socket 24 cores at 2.0 GHz, and each core has 2 FMA units of 512 bits (8 doubles) per cycle. Each node has 256 GiB of memory. Compute \(R_{\text{peak}}\). Assume that Linpack reaches 70 % of it (the efficiency of Frontier in June 2024) with a matrix that fills 80 % of the memory. Compute \(N_{\max}\) and the running time of the Linpack run.

Solution

The peak per core is \(\phi=2X\ell=2\times2\times8=32\) flop per cycle, so \(R_{\text{peak}}=100\times2\times24\times2.0\times10^{9}\times32=3.072\times10^{14}\) flop/s, 307.2 Tflop/s. At 70 %, \(R_{\max}=215.04\) Tflop/s. The memory is \(100\times256\times2^{30}=2.749\times10^{13}\) bytes, and 80 % of it holds the matrix of \(8N^2\) bytes: \(N=\sqrt{0.8\times2.749\times10^{13}/8}=1.658\times10^{6}\). The operation count is \(\tfrac23N^3=3.04\times10^{18}\) flop, and the time is \(3.04\times10^{18}/2.1504\times10^{14}=14{,}129\) s, 3.9 hours. The same arithmetic as in the listing top500_arith.py gave 2.1 hours for Frontier in June 2026: the run lasts hours because the work grows as \(N^3\) while the rate grows only with the machine.

Exercise 3 (counterexample). A student claims: "A loop of 1024 independent additions, run by 8 threads that each add 128 numbers and then one thread adding the 8 results, speeds up in proportion to the number of processing elements up to 64." Use the results of this chapter to show that the claim is false for this program, and state the largest speedup that any number of processing elements can give.

Solution

The listing workspan.py gives for this program \(W=1023\) and \(D=134\), because each thread's partial sum is a chain of 127 additions and the final sum is a chain of 7 more. By (6.4) the speedup on any number of processing elements is at most \(W/D=1023/134=7.6\). The simulated greedy times are 261 steps for \(p=4\), 134 for \(p=16\) and 134 for \(p=64\), so the speedup at \(p=64\) is \(1023/134=7.6\), not 64, and 16 processing elements already reach the limit. The claim fails because it counts independent iterations and ignores the chains that the program creates: each thread's 127 additions are dependent. The balanced tree of the same 1023 additions has \(D=10\), a bound \(W/D=102\), and a simulated speedup of \(1023/21=48.7\) at \(p=64\), still not 64, which also shows that the bound of (6.4) is not reached at every \(p\). The fix for the student's program is more partial sums or a tree, not more threads.

Exercise 4 (diagnose an assumption). A colleague computes the peak of the laptop as 12 logical processors \(\times\) 4.4 GHz \(\times\) 16 flop per cycle \(=844.8\) Gflop/s, measures 261 Gflop/s with node_scaling.c, and concludes that the program reaches 31 % of the peak. Identify the assumptions of the computation that fail and state a better bound.

Solution

Three assumptions fail. First, a logical processor is not a core. The 12 logical processors are 4 P-cores with 2 hardware threads each and 4 E-cores with 1 thread each, and the two threads of a P-core share its FMA units, so the P-cores provide 4, not 8, sets of units (6.8: a saturating thread gains \(g=1\) from a sibling). Second, the E-cores run at lower clocks (1.5 to 3.3 GHz in the third-party specification) and, in the measurement database, start one 256-bit FMA per cycle, not two, so they deliver 8 flop per cycle and at most 26.4 Gflop/s, not 70.4. Third, 4.4 GHz is the highest clock of a P-core, and the clock when all cores are active is lower; the estimates of this chapter for one thread were 3.4 to 4.2 GHz. The bound that the sources support is the sum of the per-core peaks at the highest clocks, 387.2 Gflop/s ((6.3) and the node section). Against it, 261 Gflop/s is 67 %, and the eight runs of this chapter, 196 to 284 Gflop/s, are 51 % to 73 %. The colleague's 31 % compares a measurement with a number that rests on three assumptions that do not hold, and the corrected bound is 2.2 times smaller. For this program, which gives every thread the same work and waits for the slowest, equation (6.6) gives a tighter bound: \(12\times26.4=316.8\) Gflop/s if four threads stay on E-cores, and 261 Gflop/s is 82 % of it.

Exercise 5 (design judgment). A cluster has login nodes with AVX-512 processors and compute nodes whose processors have AVX2 and FMA but no AVX-512. Users compile on the login node. Choose compiler flags for the shared build of a solver whose inner loop looks like the polynomial loop, and give the quantitative reasons.

Solution

The flag -march=native compiles for the processor on which the compiler runs. On this machine it was the only one of the five builds that produced FMA instructions (10 against 0), and it made the loop 3.5 times faster than the baseline vectorized build (58.73 against 16.86 Gflop/s). On the cluster in the question it would produce AVX-512 code on the login node, and the compute nodes do not implement those instructions; the instruction set is a contract (the section on instructions), and a binary that uses instructions outside the contract of the processor that runs it cannot be expected to run, so the job fails or must be built twice. The flags that name the lowest common set, -O3 -mavx2 -mfma, produce code that runs on both kinds of node and keeps the factor 3.5 that the baseline build gives up; a build on the login node for the compute nodes, with the flags named explicitly, also avoids the failure. The alternative of a baseline build (SSE2) is safe on every node but runs at about 1.8 to 1.9 times the scalar rate where the AVX2 build runs at 6.2 to 6.7 times, so for a solver whose time is in this loop it costs a factor 3.5 in time. The reasoning about the flag uses the observation of this chapter and the definition of an instruction set; the gcc documentation was not opened.

Exercise 6 (implementation and measurement). Change pingpong.c so that the two processes run on different logical processors, the parent on processor 2 and the child on a processor given as a third argument. Predict whether the round trip becomes shorter or longer than on one processor, and run it.

Solution

The change is two lines: int cpu = argc > 2 ? atoi(argv[2]) : 2, cpu2 = argc > 3 ? atoi(argv[3]) : cpu; and if (pid == 0) { pin(cpu2); echo_side(); _exit(0); }. A first prediction is that the round trip becomes shorter, because the two processes no longer take turns on one core and no context switch is needed. The measurement contradicts it. In the sample runs on this machine (three runs of four commands, not reproduced by a listing), the round trip on one processor was 3.1 to 6.0 µs and the round trip with the child on processor 3, 6 or 10 was 26 to 70 µs, which is 7 to 14 times longer within each run of the four commands. The probable cause, which was not tested, is that each wake-up of a process that sleeps on another processor needs a signal to that processor and a return from its idle state, and that in a virtual machine the hypervisor takes part in both, whereas on one processor the wake-up is a direct switch. The lesson is that the cost of communication between two threads depends on where they run, which is the reason for the pinning options of OpenMP (chapter 11 ff.) and MPI. The measured quantity is the round trip of a one-byte pipe exchange, and the prediction failed because it assumed that the switch was the dominant cost.
