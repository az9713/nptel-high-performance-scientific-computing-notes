What high performance scientific computing is for

A steady heat-conduction problem on a \(1000\times1000\) grid has \(n=10^{6}\) unknowns, the size that the lecture calls the smallest of practical interest. Solved by dense Gaussian elimination, it needs \(6.7\times10^{17}\) floating-point operations and 8 TB of storage. That is 7.7 days at the lecture's rate of \(10^{12}\) operations per second, and 0.55 s of arithmetic at the Linpack rate of the fastest machine on the June 2024 TOP500 list, \(1.206\times10^{18}\) operations per second. Solved by banded elimination, the same problem needs \(2\times10^{12}\) operations and 16 GB. Solved by conjugate gradients, it needs about \(9\times10^{10}\) operations and 40 MB. The operation counts of the dense and the conjugate-gradient solver differ by a factor of \(7\times10^{6}\). The rates of the lecture's machine and of that list-leading machine differ by a factor of \(1.2\times10^{6}\). The lecture concludes that direct methods make even the fastest machine useless for the simplest problems. This chapter tests that conclusion with exact operation counts, finds that it fails at \(n=10^{6}\) and holds for dense elimination from \(n=3.4\times10^{7}\), where the matrix no longer fits in memory; one day of arithmetic alone would allow \(n=5.4\times10^{7}\), so storage binds first. At \(n=10^{9}\) both limits fail: the matrix needs 8 EB, 850 times the memory, and the arithmetic needs 17.5 years, 6400 times the one-day budget. It also checks the lecture's historical and machine facts against the sources: of 17 statements, 4 are corrected, 1 is not supported and 1 is only partly supported (Table 1.3).

The objects of the chapter and the running case

A computation in this chapter is a triple: a problem, an algorithm and a machine. The chapter asks when the triple is feasible, and how the answer changes when the algorithm or the machine changes.

Problem. The running case is the steady heat (Poisson) equation \(-\Delta u=f\) on the unit square with \(u=0\) on the boundary, discretized on a grid of \(N\) interior points per side. The grid spacing is \(h=1/(N+1)\). There are \(n=N^2\) unknown values of \(u\), one per interior grid point, and the discretization gives a linear system \(A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\) with an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\). The symbol \(n\) is both the number of unknowns and the order of \(A\). The symbol \(f\) is the source term of the PDE; it is not the serial fraction of the later chapters. This is the book's running problem; chapter 9 derives its discretization error and the Jacobi iteration at full depth.

Algorithm. A flop is one floating-point operation: one addition, subtraction, multiplication or division of two 64-bit floating-point numbers. This chapter counts a multiplication and an addition as two flop even where hardware fuses them into one instruction. The work \(W\) of an algorithm on a problem is the number of flop it performs. The storage \(M\) is the number of bytes it must hold, at 8 bytes per 64-bit number. Four algorithms for \(A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\) are compared: dense LU, Gaussian elimination that treats \(A\) as a full matrix; band LU, Gaussian elimination that stores and updates only the entries within \(N\) positions of the diagonal, which is where the nonzeros and the fill-in lie when the grid points are numbered row by row (fill-in is a zero entry that becomes nonzero during elimination); Jacobi, the simplest iteration; and CG, the conjugate-gradient method. Each is defined, and its \(W\) and \(M\) derived, in the sections where the lecture introduces it or the lecture's claim about it is tested.

Machine. A machine has a rate \(R\) in flop per second, written flop/s, and a memory capacity \(M_{\text{mach}}\) in bytes. The TOP500 is a list of the 500 machines with the highest measured rate, published every six months. Two rates appear in it. \(R_{\text{peak}}\) is the theoretical maximum, computed from the number of arithmetic units, the clock and the flop per unit per cycle; chapter 6 derives it. \(R_{\max}\) is the highest rate measured while the machine runs the Linpack benchmark, defined in the section on flop/s. The later chapters of this book call a node's peak rate \(P_{\text{peak}}\); it is the same quantity as \(R_{\text{peak}}\) for one node.

Cost and feasibility. The time of the computation is modeled by \[ T=\frac{W}{R}. \tag{1.1} \] Equation (1.1) assumes three things. First, arithmetic is the only cost: memory traffic, communication and start-up cost nothing. Second, the machine sustains the rate \(R\) throughout. Third, no processing element is idle. Chapters 6 to 10 and 16 replace these assumptions with measured and modeled costs, and the section on flop/s shows one case in which the second assumption is off by a factor of about 86. A job is feasible within a time budget \(T_b\) on a machine if \(W/R\le T_b\) and \(M\le M_{\text{mach}}\). A day is \(T_b=86\,400\) s. Electrical energy is \(E=Pt\) for power \(P\) and duration \(t\).

Theory, experiment and computation: from a partial differential equation to a linear system W0L0 0:17

The lecture describes two older ways in which science advances and one newer way. In the first, observations suggest a law, and the law is then stated as a theory. In the second, a hypothesis is stated first and an experiment tests it. The lecture's examples of experiments are the Large Hadron Collider, built to test the hypothesized Higgs boson, and a water-tunnel test of the drag on a torpedo. The newer way is computation: a virtual experiment in which the laws are stated, run on a computer, and observed. The anecdotes that illustrate the first way (Archimedes and the crown, Newton and the apple) are illustrations in the lecture, and this chapter uses none of them as evidence.

The lecture's central claim about computation is that the laws of physics are usually partial differential equations (PDEs), that these usually have no closed-form solution, and that a computer cannot work on a PDE directly. The PDE must first be converted into algebraic equations. The following construction shows the conversion for the running case and fixes what the machine receives.

Let \(u(x,y)\) be smooth, and let \(h\gt0\). The Taylor expansions of \(u(x\pm h,y)\) about \((x,y)\) add to \[ u(x+h,y)+u(x-h,y)-2u(x,y)=h^2u_{xx}(x,y)+\tfrac{1}{12}h^4u_{xxxx}(x,y)+\cdots, \] because the odd-order terms cancel. The same holds in the \(y\) direction. Adding the two results and rearranging gives the five-point approximation of the Laplacian \[ -\Delta u(x,y)=\frac{4u(x,y)-u(x-h,y)-u(x+h,y)-u(x,y-h)-u(x,y+h)}{h^2}+O(h^2). \tag{1.2} \] The term \(O(h^2)\), the truncation error of the stencil, has the leading part \(-\tfrac{1}{12}h^2(u_{xxxx}+u_{yyyy})\). Now let the unknown \(u_{i,j}\) stand for the value of \(u\) at the grid point \((ih,jh)\), for \(1\le i,j\le N\). Drop the \(O(h^2)\) term, set the left side equal to \(f(ih,jh)\), and treat any neighbor outside the grid as a boundary value, which is zero. The result is one linear equation per grid point, \[ 4u_{i,j}-u_{i-1,j}-u_{i+1,j}-u_{i,j-1}-u_{i,j+1}=h^2f(ih,jh), \] so \(N^2\) equations in \(N^2\) unknowns: the system \(A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\) of the model section. Each row of \(A\) has at most five nonzero entries. The count of nonzeros follows from the grid: there are \(N^2\) diagonal entries, and the \(N(N-1)\) horizontal and \(N(N-1)\) vertical pairs of neighboring points each contribute two off-diagonal entries, so \(A\) has \(N^2+4N(N-1)=5N^2-4N\) nonzeros among \(N^4\) entries. A matrix with so few nonzeros is sparse. Sparsity is the property that the later sections exploit.

The truncation error of the stencil is not yet the error of the computed solution. That the computed \(u_{i,j}\) differ from the exact solution by \(O(h^2)\) needs a stability argument, which chapter 9 gives. The next listing tests the claim on one case and counts the nonzeros. Input: the grid sizes \(N=7,15,31\), so \(h=1/8,1/16,1/32\). Method: the program builds the matrix \(A\) with Kronecker products, chooses the exact solution \(u(x,y)=\sin\pi x\,\sin\pi y\), computes the matching source \(f=2\pi^2\sin\pi x\,\sin\pi y\), solves the system with the dense LAPACK solver of NumPy, and compares the result with the exact values at the grid points. Output: the number of nonzeros against \(5N^2-4N\), the largest error, and the ratio of the error to the error on the previous, coarser grid. Assumption: the solution is smooth. The run checks the nonzero count exactly and shows the rate of decrease of the error.

"""Check the 5-point discretization of -Laplace(u) = f on the unit square: nonzero count and error against a known solution."""
import math
import numpy as np

def matrix(N):
    """Dense 5-point matrix: 4 on the diagonal, -1 to each grid neighbour (row-major ordering)."""
    T = 2.0 * np.eye(N) - np.eye(N, k=1) - np.eye(N, k=-1)
    return np.kron(np.eye(N), T) + np.kron(T, np.eye(N))

def apply(N, u):
    """Matrix-free A u for u of shape (N, N)."""
    p = np.zeros((N + 2, N + 2)); p[1:-1, 1:-1] = u
    return 4.0 * u - p[:-2, 1:-1] - p[2:, 1:-1] - p[1:-1, :-2] - p[1:-1, 2:]

def rhs(N):
    h = 1.0 / (N + 1)
    x = np.arange(1, N + 1) * h
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, x, indexing="ij")
    exact = np.sin(math.pi * X) * np.sin(math.pi * Y)
    return h * h * 2.0 * math.pi ** 2 * exact, exact

print("N   nnz  5N^2-4N  max error   error ratio to previous N")
prev = None
for N in (7, 15, 31):
    A = matrix(N); b, exact = rhs(N)
    u = np.linalg.solve(A, b.ravel()).reshape(N, N)
    err = np.max(np.abs(u - exact))
    print(f"{N:<3} {np.count_nonzero(A):<5} {5*N*N - 4*N:<8} {err:.3e}  {'' if prev is None else f'{prev / err:.2f}'}")
    prev = err

The nonzero counts equal \(5N^2-4N\) for all three grids. The largest error is \(1.295\times10^{-2}\), \(3.219\times10^{-3}\) and \(8.036\times10^{-4}\), and each halving of \(h\) divides it by 4.02 and then 4.01. An error proportional to \(h^2\) divides by 4 when \(h\) halves, so the data agree with second-order accuracy. The test has limits. It uses one smooth solution on one square domain, so it does not show the order for a source with a singularity or for a domain with a re-entrant corner, where solutions are known to be less smooth. It also uses a source built from a known solution, which is the opposite of the situation that the lecture describes: for the unit square with a smooth source the solution has a series form, so the lecture's statement that PDEs usually lack closed forms applies to equations with nonlinear terms, variable coefficients or complicated geometry, which this model problem does not have. The model problem serves as a case whose cost can be counted exactly.

The size of the system follows from the resolution that the application needs. A grid with \(N\) points per side resolves features of size about \(1/N\) of the domain. The case \(n=10^{6}\) needs \(N=1000\) in two dimensions, or \(N=100\) in three. The lecture's board lists the range of unknowns as \(10^{6}\) to \(10^{12}\); in three dimensions \(n=10^{12}\) means \(N=10^{4}\), and in two dimensions it means \(N=10^{6}\). The count of unknowns, and with it the cost of the solution, grows as a power of the resolution. That growth is the reason that the lecture turns next to the cost of solving \(A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\).

Four contributors and what the sources record W0L0 6:35

The lecture names four people and says that the selection is not exhaustive. Each stands for one stage of the path from a physical law to a number: a model of computation (Turing), an organization of a machine (von Neumann), a family of solvers (Krylov) and a family of discretizations (Galerkin). For each person this section gives the lecture's statement, what the sources opened for this chapter record, and the consequence. Where the two disagree, the chapter follows the source and says so.

Turing. The lecture says that Turing proposed an abstract computer in which one bit of information enters a black box, is modified by operations, and leaves as output; and that this model underlies general-purpose computing. Turing's paper, On computable numbers, with an application to the Entscheidungsproblem, was received on 28 May 1936 and read on 12 November 1936 (Proc. London Math. Soc. s2-42, pp. 230–265, copy read from p. 230). Its machine has a tape divided into sections, called squares, each able to bear a symbol, and the machine acts on the scanned symbol. The introduction states that the paper gives arguments for the claim that the computable numbers include all numbers that could naturally be regarded as computable. The lecture's one-bit description fits a single step of that machine more than the whole model. The consequence for this chapter is a limit, not a quantity. The model defines what can be computed. It assigns no cost to a computation. The time \(T=W/R\) of equation (1.1) is a different object: it describes a physical machine and depends on how fast that machine executes operations.

von Neumann. The lecture says that von Neumann designed some of the first general-purpose computers on the architecture that bears his name, that this architecture lets the Turing model run on hardware, and that this work was done about 80 years ago. The title page of the First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC carries the date 30 June 1945, names a contract between the United States Army Ordnance Department and the University of Pennsylvania, and appears in a reprint with an introduction by Godfrey (IEEE Annals of the History of Computing 15(4), 1993, from p. 27). Only the first two pages of this scanned copy were read, because the file has no text layer. The date and the contract are therefore the only facts that the chapter takes from it. The report describes a proposed machine, the EDVAC, so the chapter does not decide whether it was among the first general-purpose computers; that needs sources that were not opened. The separation of a processor from a memory, which the lecture attributes to this architecture, returns in chapters 7 and 10, because the cost of moving data between the two is the first assumption of equation (1.1) that fails.

Krylov (W0L0 9:55). The lecture calls Alexei Krylov a decorated military officer at the end of the 19th century, credits him with iterative solvers for linear systems that reduce the cost from \(O(n^3)\) to \(O(n\log n)\), and says that these methods were developed at the turn of the century. The MacTutor biography of Aleksei Krylov (1863–1945) records that his father was a retired artillery officer; that Krylov taught at the naval academy for about fifty years and trained a school of shipbuilders; that he received a gold medal of the Royal Institution of Naval Architects for work on the oscillation of ships; and that on 3 January 1900 he was a Captain of the Admiralty when he was made acting superintendent of the Towing Tank, and that in January 1908 he was a General and was appointed Chief Naval Ship Construction Inspector. The description as a decorated officer agrees with MacTutor; his service was naval. The English Wikipedia article on Aleksey Krylov records the Imperial Russian Navy as his branch, and a paper of 1931 on the characteristic polynomial of a matrix, in which the work is counted as a number of multiplications (Izvestiya Akademii Nauk SSSR 7(4), pp. 491–539, cited there; the original was not opened). So Krylov was a naval engineer and mathematician, the date of his best-known mathematical paper is 1931, and the paper treats an eigenvalue problem, not the solution of \(A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\).

The link to linear systems is the subspace that bears his name. For a matrix \(A\) and a vector \(\mathbf{r}\), the Krylov subspace of order \(m\) is \(\mathcal{K}_m(A,\mathbf{r})=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{r},A\mathbf{r},\dots,A^{m-1}\mathbf{r}\}\). A Krylov method looks for an approximate solution of \(A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\) inside such a subspace. The earliest source opened here that gives a Krylov-subspace solver is Hestenes and Stiefel (J. Res. Nat. Bur. Standards 49(6), December 1952, pp. 409–436). Their abstract describes an iterative algorithm for \(n\) equations in \(n\) unknowns whose solution is obtained in \(n\) steps, and shows that it is a special case of a general method that includes Gaussian elimination. Their introduction states that C. Lanczos had recently developed a closely related method. The date of the Krylov solvers in the sources opened is therefore 1952, not the turn of the century. A further fact bears on the lecture's use of the word iterative. Shewchuk (notes on the conjugate-gradient method, 1994, p. 33) states that the mathematical community discarded conjugate gradients during the 1960s, because rounding errors destroy the finite termination in \(n\) steps, and that interest returned when evidence appeared in the 1970s that the method works well as an iterative procedure that stops long before \(n\) steps. The cost claim \(O(n\log n)\) is tested in its own section below.

Galerkin (W0L0 17:01). The lecture calls Boris Galerkin an admiral of the Soviet Navy and credits him with a family of techniques that convert PDEs into algebraic equations. The MacTutor biography of Boris Galerkin (1871–1945) describes a Soviet mathematician and engineer, born in Polotsk, who worked as an engineer at a locomotive plant and a boiler plant and taught at the Petersburg Technological Institute, and whose method of approximate integration of differential equations the biography dates to 1915. The page records no rank. English Wikipedia, a secondary source, records that in 1939 Galerkin became head of the structural mechanics department of the Navy's Higher Engineering-Technical School (VITU) with the rank of lieutenant general, without earlier military service. No source opened records the rank of admiral, so the chapter does not repeat it. The method itself can be stated for the model problem. Choose \(n\) basis functions \(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_n\) that vanish on the boundary and seek \(u_h=\sum_j u_j\phi_j\). Require that the residual \(-\Delta u_h-f\) is orthogonal to every basis function: \(\int(-\Delta u_h-f)\,\phi_i=0\) for \(i=1,\dots,n\). Integration by parts turns this into \(\sum_j\bigl(\int\nabla\phi_i\cdot\nabla\phi_j\bigr)u_j=\int f\phi_i\), which is again a linear system \(K\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\), sparse when each \(\phi_i\) is nonzero on a small region. This is the standard definition; no textbook was opened for it. The finite-difference system of the running case was not derived as a Galerkin system, but its matrix equals the Galerkin matrix of piecewise-linear basis functions on the grid triangulated by one diagonal per square, with a different right side (a standard result; no finite-element text was opened for it). The cost analysis of the next sections applies to both.

The cost of a direct solve W0L0 10:31

The lecture writes the solution of \(A\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\) as \(\mathbf{u}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b}\), transforms a \(3\times3\) matrix to upper triangular form on the board, and states that the number of operations of such a direct method is of order \(n^3\). For \(n=3\) it gives about \(3^3=27\) operations. For a million unknowns it gives \(10^{18}\) operations, which at \(10^{12}\) flop/s is \(10^{6}\) seconds to solution, a time that the lecture calls inordinately large. The board shows the entries \(10^{12}\) to \(10^{15}\) for the rates of large machines and \(10^{6}\) to \(10^{12}\) for the numbers of unknowns. A direct method is an algorithm that produces the solution, apart from rounding error, after a fixed finite number of operations.

The order \(n^3\) hides a constant, and for the lecture's argument the constant matters. The derivation below gives the exact count for Gaussian elimination. The factorization \(A=LU\), with \(L\) unit lower triangular and \(U\) upper triangular, proceeds in \(n-1\) steps. At step \(k\), let \(m=n-k\) be the number of rows below row \(k\). The method forms \(m\) multipliers \(\ell_{ik}=a_{ik}/a_{kk}\), which costs \(m\) divisions, and then updates the \(m\times m\) block of remaining entries by \(a_{ij}\leftarrow a_{ij}-\ell_{ik}a_{kj}\), which costs one multiplication and one subtraction per entry, \(2m^2\) flop. The sum over the steps is \[ W_{LU}(n)=\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\bigl(m+2m^2\bigr)=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}+\frac{(n-1)n(2n-1)}{3}=\frac{n(n-1)(4n+1)}{6}=\tfrac{2}{3}n^3+O(n^2). \tag{1.3} \] Given \(L\) and \(U\), the solution of \(LU\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{b}\) takes a forward substitution with \(n(n-1)\) flop and a backward substitution with \(n(n-1)+n\) flop, in total \(2n^2-n\). Partial pivoting, the choice of the largest available entry as the pivot, adds comparisons and row exchanges, which are not flop, and leaves the leading term unchanged. The Linpack benchmark of the TOP500 relies on this fact: the TOP500 description of the benchmark requires an algorithm that conforms to LU factorization with partial pivoting and states that the operation count must be \(\tfrac23n^3+O(n^2)\) double-precision operations. The reference implementation HPL computes its reported rate from \(\tfrac23n^3+\tfrac32n^2\) (HPL 2.3, testing/ptest/HPL_pdtest.c, lines 231–233). The lecture's route through the explicit inverse \(A^{-1}\) is not the algorithm that the benchmark uses; the chapter does not state the cost of forming an inverse, because no source for it was opened.

The next listing counts. Input: the sizes \(n=3,10,100,300\). Method: every addition, subtraction, multiplication and division of the program passes through one of four functions that increment a counter, so the count is exact and independent of the compiler. The program factorizes a diagonally dominant matrix, which needs no pivoting, solves one system with the factors, and multiplies two matrices with the textbook triple loop. Output: the counted flop of each task, the closed form (1.3), and the ratio of the factorization count to \(\tfrac23n^3\). The run checks (1.3) exactly.

#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>

/* Every floating-point +, -, *, / goes through one of these four counters. */
static unsigned long long flops = 0;
static double add(double a, double b) { flops++; return a + b; }
static double sub(double a, double b) { flops++; return a - b; }
static double mul(double a, double b) { flops++; return a * b; }
static double dvd(double a, double b) { flops++; return a / b; }

/* LU factorization without pivoting, in place: A = L U, unit lower L. */
static void lu(int n, double *a) {
    for (int k = 0; k < n - 1; k++)
        for (int i = k + 1; i < n; i++) {
            a[i*n + k] = dvd(a[i*n + k], a[k*n + k]);
            for (int j = k + 1; j < n; j++)
                a[i*n + j] = sub(a[i*n + j], mul(a[i*n + k], a[k*n + j]));
        }
}

/* Forward and back substitution for L U x = b, in place in b. */
static void lu_solve(int n, const double *a, double *b) {
    for (int i = 1; i < n; i++)
        for (int j = 0; j < i; j++) b[i] = sub(b[i], mul(a[i*n + j], b[j]));
    for (int i = n - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
        for (int j = i + 1; j < n; j++) b[i] = sub(b[i], mul(a[i*n + j], b[j]));
        b[i] = dvd(b[i], a[i*n + i]);
    }
}

/* C = A B, all n x n. */
static void matmul(int n, const double *a, const double *b, double *c) {
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
        for (int j = 0; j < n; j++) {
            double s = 0.0;
            for (int k = 0; k < n; k++) s = add(s, mul(a[i*n + k], b[k*n + j]));
            c[i*n + j] = s;
        }
}

int main(void) {
    int sizes[] = {3, 10, 100, 300};
    printf("%5s %12s %12s %12s %12s %10s\n", "n", "LU counted", "formula", "solve cnt", "matmul cnt", "LU/(2n^3/3)");
    for (int s = 0; s < 4; s++) {
        int n = sizes[s];
        double *a = malloc(sizeof(double) * n * n), *b = malloc(sizeof(double) * n * n);
        double *c = malloc(sizeof(double) * n * n), *x = malloc(sizeof(double) * n);
        for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) {
            x[i] = 1.0;
            for (int j = 0; j < n; j++) a[i*n + j] = b[i*n + j] = (i == j) ? n + 1.0 : 1.0 / (1 + i + j);
        }
        flops = 0; lu(n, a);               unsigned long long f_lu = flops;
        flops = 0; lu_solve(n, a, x);      unsigned long long f_sv = flops;
        flops = 0; matmul(n, b, b, c);     unsigned long long f_mm = flops;
        unsigned long long formula = (unsigned long long)n * (n - 1) * (4ULL * n + 1) / 6;
        printf("%5d %12llu %12llu %12llu %12llu %10.4f\n", n, f_lu, formula, f_sv, f_mm,
               (double)f_lu / (2.0 * n * n * n / 3.0));
        free(a); free(b); free(c); free(x);
    }
    return 0;
}

The matrix of the running case is not dense, and elimination can use that. With the grid points numbered row by row, the entry \(a_{ij}\) of \(A\) is nonzero only if the points \(i\) and \(j\) are neighbors, so the nonzeros lie within \(N\) positions of the diagonal. Elimination without pivoting creates no nonzero outside that band. (Demmel's CS267 notes for lectures 15 and 16 states this for the model problem: entries more than \(N\) positions from the diagonal never fill in.) At each of the \(n\) steps, only the \(N\times N\) block below and to the right of the pivot is updated, at \(2N^2\) flop, so \[ W_{\text{band}}\approx 2nN^2=2N^4,\qquad M_{\text{band}}\approx 8n(2N+1)\ \text{bytes}. \tag{1.5} \] For \(N=1000\), this is \(2\times10^{12}\) flop and 16 GB, against \(6.7\times10^{17}\) flop and 8 TB for dense elimination. The factor between the two is \(3.3\times10^{5}\) in operations and \(5\times10^{2}\) in storage. The factor of \(3.3\times10^{5}\) in operations that the structure of the matrix gives is 0.28 of the factor of \(1.2\times10^{6}\) between a machine of \(10^{12}\) flop/s and the June 2024 list leader.

Flop/s, Rpeak, Rmax, and what the TOP500 measures W0L0 14:06

The lecture defines the unit of machine speed as FLOPS, floating-point operations per second, where one operation is one addition, subtraction, multiplication or division, and says that large machines perform \(10^{12}\) to \(10^{15}\) of them per second. This book writes flop for a count of operations and flop/s for a rate, because the capital-letter form of the lecture serves both meanings. The prefixes are \(\mathrm{G}=10^{9}\), \(\mathrm{T}=10^{12}\), \(\mathrm{P}=10^{15}\) and \(\mathrm{E}=10^{18}\), so 1 Gflop/s is \(10^{9}\) flop/s and 1 Eflop/s is \(10^{18}\) flop/s. The lecture does not say how a rate is measured. The measurement defines the number, and the TOP500 list, which the lecture shows later, uses one specific measurement.

The TOP500 description of the Linpack benchmark states the procedure. The benchmark solves a dense system of linear equations. The submitter may scale the size \(n\) of the system and optimize the software for the machine. The algorithm must be LU factorization with partial pivoting, with an operation count of \(\tfrac23n^3+O(n^2)\); this excludes fast matrix multiplication, lower-precision solves followed by iterative refinement, and software emulation of floating point. The rate at the best problem size \(N_{\max}\) is \(R_{\max}\). The problem size at which half of \(R_{\max}\) is reached is \(N_{1/2}\). The theoretical peak \(R_{\text{peak}}\) is listed beside it. The description adds that the number does not reflect the overall performance of a system, that it reflects the performance of a dedicated system for solving a dense system of linear equations, and that, because the problem is very regular, the achieved performance is quite high. Two consequences follow for this chapter. First, \(R_{\max}\) is measured on the algorithm that the lecture calls intractable for large \(n\): the list ranks machines by dense Gaussian elimination, with the size chosen to suit the machine. Second, a time estimate \(W/R_{\max}\) for any other algorithm assumes that the machine runs that algorithm as fast as it runs dense elimination.

#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <math.h>
#include <omp.h>

#define BI 64
#define BK 256
#define BJ 512
#define MIN(a, b) ((a) < (b) ? (a) : (b))

/* C = A B for n x n row-major matrices; i-j-k loops, no blocking. */
static void naive(int n, const double *a, const double *b, double *c) {
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
        for (int j = 0; j < n; j++) {
            double s = 0.0;
            for (int k = 0; k < n; k++) s += a[(size_t)i*n + k] * b[(size_t)k*n + j];
            c[(size_t)i*n + j] = s;
        }
}

/* Same product with tiles of BI x BK x BJ and the row blocks shared among threads. */
static void blocked(int n, const double *a, const double *b, double *c) {
    for (size_t t = 0; t < (size_t)n * n; t++) c[t] = 0.0;
    #pragma omp parallel for schedule(static)
    for (int ii = 0; ii < n; ii += BI)
        for (int kk = 0; kk < n; kk += BK)
            for (int jj = 0; jj < n; jj += BJ)
                for (int i = ii; i < MIN(ii + BI, n); i++)
                    for (int k = kk; k < MIN(kk + BK, n); k++) {
                        double aik = a[(size_t)i*n + k];
                        for (int j = jj; j < MIN(jj + BJ, n); j++)
                            c[(size_t)i*n + j] += aik * b[(size_t)k*n + j];
                    }
}

static double best_time(void (*f)(int, const double *, const double *, double *),
                        int n, const double *a, const double *b, double *c, int reps) {
    double best = 1e30;
    for (int r = 0; r < reps; r++) {
        double t0 = omp_get_wtime(); f(n, a, b, c); double t = omp_get_wtime() - t0;
        if (t < best) best = t;
    }
    return best;
}

int main(void) {

    double *a = malloc(sizeof(double) * n * n), *b = malloc(sizeof(double) * n * n);

Iterative methods and the claim of \(O(n\log n)\) W0L0 15:57

The lecture's remedy for the cost of direct solvers is the iterative method: guess a solution, improve the guess, and stop when it is good enough. The board shows the cost falling from \(O(n^3)\) to \(O(n\log n)\), labeled with the name Krylov and the words subspace solvers. The lecture adds that without iterative methods, modern computers could not produce meaningful results for problems of this size. An iterative method produces a sequence of approximations that converge to the solution; the number of iterations needed depends on the matrix and on the accuracy required. This section derives the cost of two iterative methods on the running case and tests the lecture's formula against the table of a numerical-analysis course.

Jacobi. The Jacobi method updates every unknown from the values of its four neighbors at the previous sweep: \(u_{i,j}\leftarrow\tfrac14\bigl(b_{i,j}+u_{i-1,j}+u_{i+1,j}+u_{i,j-1}+u_{i,j+1}\bigr)\). A sweep costs 5 flop per unknown, four additions and one multiplication, so \(5n\) flop. The error of the iterate shrinks by a factor \(\rho\) per sweep, and for the model problem \(\rho=\cos\bigl(\pi/(N+1)\bigr)\) (stated in Demmel's CS267 notes for lectures 15 and 16; chapter 9 derives it). Since \(\rho\approx1-\pi^2/\bigl(2(N+1)^2\bigr)\), reducing the error by a factor \(\varepsilon\) takes \[ k_{\text{Jacobi}}=\frac{\ln\varepsilon}{\ln\rho}\approx\frac{2(N+1)^2}{\pi^2}\ln\frac1\varepsilon \tag{1.6} \] sweeps. The total work \(5nk\) grows as \(nN^2=n^2\). Jacobi needs \(O(n^2)\) flop, one power of \(n\) fewer than dense elimination and the same order as band elimination.

Conjugate gradients. The conjugate-gradient method (CG) applies to a symmetric positive definite matrix, a class that contains the matrix \(A\) of the running case. After \(k\) iterations it holds the approximation inside the Krylov subspace \(\mathcal{K}_k(A,\mathbf{b})\) for which the error in the energy norm \(\lVert\mathbf{e}\rVert_A=\sqrt{\mathbf{e}^{T}A\mathbf{e}}\) is smallest (Shewchuk, p. 33, and Hestenes and Stiefel). One iteration costs one product of \(A\) with a vector, two inner products and three vector updates. For the five-point matrix this is about \(20n\) flop. Shewchuk (Section 10, p. 37) shows that reducing the error norm by a factor \(\varepsilon\) takes at most \[ k_{\text{CG}}\le\Bigl\lceil\tfrac12\sqrt{\kappa}\,\ln\frac2\varepsilon\Bigr\rceil \tag{1.7} \] iterations, where \(\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) is the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue of \(A\), the condition number. The eigenvalues of the five-point matrix are \(4-2\cos(i\pi h)-2\cos(j\pi h)\) for \(1\le i,j\le N\) (chapter 9 derives this), so \(\kappa=\cot^2\bigl(\pi h/2\bigr)\approx\bigl(2(N+1)/\pi\bigr)^2\) and \(\sqrt\kappa\approx0.64\,(N+1)\). The iteration count of CG therefore grows in proportion to \(N=\sqrt n\), the work is \(20n\cdot O(N)=O(n^{3/2})\), and the storage is five vectors, \(40n\) bytes. Shewchuk states the general result on p. 38: CG needs \(O(m\sqrt\kappa)\) operations for a matrix with \(m\) nonzeros; for finite-difference and finite-element problems \(\kappa\in O(n^{2/d})\) in \(d\) dimensions, so CG costs \(O(n^{3/2})\) in two dimensions and \(O(n^{4/3})\) in three.

Input: the source term \(f=1\), for which \(\mathbf{b}\) is not an eigenvector of \(A\) (a source proportional to an eigenvector would let CG finish in one iteration, which hides the dependence on \(N\)); grid sizes \(N=8,16,32\) for Jacobi and \(N=16,32,64,128\) for CG. """Count Jacobi and conjugate-gradient iterations for the 5-point Poisson system on an N x N grid."""
import math
import numpy as np

TOL = 1e-6

def apply(N, u):
    """Matrix-free A u for u of shape (N, N): 4 on the diagonal, -1 to each grid neighbour."""
    p = np.zeros((N + 2, N + 2)); p[1:-1, 1:-1] = u
    return 4.0 * u - p[:-2, 1:-1] - p[2:, 1:-1] - p[1:-1, :-2] - p[1:-1, 2:]

def source_one(N):
    """Right-hand side of -Laplace(u) = 1, scaled by h^2. It is not an eigenvector of A."""
    return np.full((N, N), (1.0 / (N + 1)) ** 2)

def jacobi_iterations(N):
    b = source_one(N); u = np.zeros((N, N)); nb = np.linalg.norm(b); k = 0
    while np.linalg.norm(b - apply(N, u)) > TOL * nb:
        u = u + (b - apply(N, u)) / 4.0; k += 1
    return k

def cg_iterations(N):
    b = source_one(N); u = np.zeros((N, N)); r = b.copy(); p = r.copy()
    rr = np.sum(r * r); nb = math.sqrt(rr); k = 0
    while math.sqrt(rr) > TOL * nb:
        Ap = apply(N, p); alpha = rr / np.sum(p * Ap)
        u += alpha * p; r -= alpha * Ap
        rr_new = np.sum(r * r); p = r + (rr_new / rr) * p; rr = rr_new; k += 1
    return k

The lecture turns to the first computing machines. It says that a search for the first computer returns Babbage's Difference Engine, a machine with a specific purpose: to find the coefficients of the polynomial fitted to a set of data. It then names ENIAC as the first large-scale general-purpose computer, built from vacuum tubes, housed in a large room and consuming much power. The lecture states that ENIAC performed about 300 floating-point operations per second and that it was used to compute artillery firing tables, which, according to the lecture, required the full three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations. The sources opened support the purpose and the scale of these machines, correct the purpose of the Difference Engine, refine the rate of ENIAC, and do not support the claim about the Navier–Stokes equations.

The Difference Engine. The Science Museum Group page on Babbage's Difference Engines describes Difference Engine No. 1 as a machine built to calculate a series of numerical values and to print the results automatically, not to perform everyday arithmetic. It states that Babbage used the principle of finite differences, which needs only addition and subtraction and which human computers used to prepare mathematical tables. The page records that a portion of the engine, one seventh of the complete design, was completed in 1832, that work stopped in 1833, and that the Treasury had spent £17 500. The machine therefore tabulated the values of a given polynomial. It did not compute the coefficients of a polynomial from data, which is the lecture's description.

The method of finite differences shows why addition suffices. For a function \(p\) tabulated at equally spaced points, the first difference is \(\Delta p(x)=p(x+1)-p(x)\), the second difference is \(\Delta^2p(x)=\Delta p(x+1)-\Delta p(x)\), and so on. For a polynomial of degree \(d\), the difference \(\Delta^dp\) is a constant. Each new table entry then follows from the previous entries by \(d\) additions: \(p(x+1)=p(x)+\Delta p(x)\), \(\Delta p(x+1)=\Delta p(x)+\Delta^2p(x)\), up to the constant. As an example, take \(p(x)=x^2+x+41\) (the polynomial is this chapter's example, not Babbage's). The values for \(x=0,\dots,5\) are 41, 43, 47, 53, 61, 71; the first differences are 2, 4, 6, 8, 10; the second difference is 2 throughout. The next entry is \(71+(10+2)=83\), which equals \(6^2+6+41\). A degree-2 table needs 2 additions per entry and no multiplication. The cost of an operation, which equation (1.1) treats as uniform, was in 1832 a mechanical design problem, and Babbage chose a method that avoided the costly operation.

ENIAC. Weik's survey, The ENIAC Story (Ordnance Ballistic Research Laboratories, 1961), expands the name as electronic numerical integrator and computer. (The slide of the lecture shows only the name ENIAC and the number 300.) It records that the machine was formally dedicated at the Moore School of the University of Pennsylvania on 15 February 1946; that it had thirty units weighing more than thirty tons; that its 19 000 vacuum tubes, 1 500 relays and other components consumed almost 200 kilowatts; and that it was developed by Army Ordnance to compute ballistic firing tables, which the Ballistic Research Laboratory at Aberdeen prepared. The lecture's statements about the purpose, the vacuum tubes and the power are consistent with Weik.

Weik also gives the timing, which fixes the rate of the machine. ENIAC's twenty accumulators, its arithmetic and storage units, each held a 10-digit decimal number. The cycling unit emitted pulses at 10-microsecond intervals, and one addition took 200 microseconds. A multiplication of two 10-digit numbers took 2.6 milliseconds. Division and square-root extraction took 25 milliseconds on average for a 10-digit number. The rates are therefore \(1/200\ \mu\mathrm{s}=5000\) additions per second, \(1/2.6\ \mathrm{ms}=385\) multiplications per second and \(1/25\ \mathrm{ms}=40\) divisions per second. The lecture's 300 operations per second lies between the division rate and the multiplication rate and is within a factor of 1.3 of the multiplication rate, so it is correct as an order of magnitude. Weik describes decimal numbers of 10 digits and does not describe a floating-point format. The operations of ENIAC are therefore not flop in the sense of the model section, which counts operations on 64-bit floating-point numbers. The comparison of ENIAC with a modern machine, made in the next section, compares different operations and is meaningful only to within an order of magnitude.

The firing tables. The lecture's account of the problem is accurate in structure. A soldier at the front can measure the distance and elevation of the target and the wind and temperature, and can set only two angles, so the calculation must be done before the soldier needs it and handed over as a table that the soldier reads. This is the earliest instance in the lecture of a pattern that recurs through the book: a large precomputation, whose cost is paid once, serves many later uses. The cost of such a table, in the model of this chapter, is the product of the numbers of tabulated values of each input, multiplied by the work \(W_{\text{traj}}\) of one trajectory. As an illustration with assumed numbers, four inputs with 10 values each and \(W_{\text{traj}}=10^{3}\) multiplications make \(10^{7}\) multiplications, which is \(10^{7}/385=2.6\times10^{4}\) s, or 7.2 hours, at the multiplication rate of ENIAC computed above. The numbers are assumptions chosen to show the arithmetic; they are not data about the tables of the Ballistic Research Laboratory. The lecture's physical claim, that each entry required the full three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations, has no support in Weik. His account describes the earlier differential analyzer, a machine with ten integrating units at Aberdeen, failing during a long "trajectory run", and it describes ENIAC's early problems as numerical solutions in fields such as atomic energy and ballistic trajectories. A trajectory is computed by integrating the motion of one projectile. Weik does not mention the Navier–Stokes equations, and the chapter does not repeat the claim.

An exponential growth law has a doubling time \(T_2\). For a quantity that has the value \(v_0\) at time \(t_0\) and \(v_1\) at time \(t_1\), and that grows at a constant exponential rate, the doubling time is \[ T_2=\frac{t_1-t_0}{\log_2(v_1/v_0)}. \tag{1.8} \] The following listing computes (1.8) for the values of Table 1.1, for four more rank-1 entries of other lists, and for ENIAC. Moore's observation. The quantity that doubled in the first, best-known statement is not an operation rate. Moore (Electronics, 19 April 1965, pp. 114–117, read in the reprint of Proc. IEEE 86(1), 1998, pp. 82–85) writes that the complexity for minimum component costs, the number of components per integrated circuit at which the cost per component is lowest, had increased at roughly a factor of two per year, that this rate could be expected to continue for at least ten years, and that the number of components per circuit at minimum cost would then be 65 000 in 1975. A factor of two per year for ten years is \(2^{10}=1024\), so the statement implies about 63 components per circuit in 1965. Components per circuit is the count of transistors and similar parts. A machine's rate in flop/s also depends on how fast the components switch and on how many can operate at once. Dennard et al. addressed that.

Dennard scaling. Dennard and co-authors (IEEE J. Solid-State Circuits 9(5), 1974, pp. 256–268, read in the National Academies reprint of 2011 and in a scanned copy) state the rules by which a MOSFET switch, the basic transistor of digital circuits, is reduced in size. Each linear dimension and the voltage are divided by a factor \(s\gt1\) (Dennard et al. write \(\kappa\); this chapter writes \(s\), because \(\kappa\) is the condition number of the section on iterative methods), and the doping concentration of the substrate is multiplied by \(s\). Their Table I lists the results: the capacitance and the delay of a circuit fall by \(s\), the power dissipation of a circuit falls by \(s^2\), and the power density stays constant. The text states the consequence: even if many more circuits are placed on a chip, the cooling problem is essentially unchanged. The same result follows from the switching power of one circuit, \(P=CV^2f_{\text{clk}}\), where \(C\) is its capacitance, \(V\) the supply voltage and \(f_{\text{clk}}\) the clock frequency, if every circuit switches once per cycle. The capacitance of a circuit is \(C=\epsilon_{\text{ox}}A_{\text{g}}/t_{\text{ox}}\), with gate area \(A_{\text{g}}\), insulator thickness \(t_{\text{ox}}\) and permittivity \(\epsilon_{\text{ox}}\), so it falls by \(s^2/s\), that is by \(s\); the frequency is the inverse of the delay and rises by \(s\). The circuits per unit of chip area rise by \(s^2\). Then \[ \frac{P_{\text{new}}}{P_{\text{old}}}=\frac1s\cdot\frac1{s^2}\cdot s=\frac1{s^2}, \qquad \frac{\text{power per unit area, new}}{\text{power per unit area, old}}=\frac1{s^2}\cdot s^2=1. \tag{1.9} \] The last table of the listing above evaluates (1.9) for one generation, \(s=\sqrt2\), which halves the area of a circuit. With the voltage scaled as Dennard prescribes, the power per area is 1.000 of its old value. With the voltage held fixed and the frequency raised by \(s\), it is 2.000. With both held fixed, it is 1.414. A chip can dissipate only a limited power per area, so a designer who cannot lower the voltage must give up the rise of the frequency, the use of all circuits at once, or both. Dennard et al. also state that the subthreshold behavior of the device, the current that flows when the switch is nominally off, does not scale as desired. The sources opened do not show when or whether that stopped the lowering of the voltage; chapters 9 and 31 treat the end of Dennard scaling and its consequences at the depth that the question needs.

The list data fit the consequence. Between June 2010 and June 2024, the Rmax of rank 1 rose by a factor of 686, the number of listed cores by 38.8, and the clock frequency named in the entry of the leader fell from 2.6 GHz (Jaguar, Opteron) to 2.0 GHz (Frontier, EPYC). The Rmax per listed core rose from \(7.85\times10^{9}\) to \(1.39\times10^{11}\) flop/s, a factor of 17.7; the later count of cores includes the compute units of the graphics processors, as the section on Frontier shows, so this factor combines more work per cycle with a different kind of core. The data show that the rise of the leader's rate after 2010 came from more processing elements, of a different kind in 2024, with a clock that did not rise. They do not separate the effect of power limits from that of other causes. The consequence for this book is stated and not proved here: a machine of \(10^{18}\) flop/s is a machine of millions of processing elements, and the programs of chapters 11 to 26 exist to use them.

Exercise 1 (derive). Derive the operation count of band LU factorization for a matrix of order \(n\) with half-bandwidth \(b\) (all nonzeros within \(b\) positions of the diagonal, no fill-in outside the band). Specialize to the five-point matrix with \(n=N^2\) and \(b=N\), compute the ratio of this count to the dense count \(\tfrac23n^3\) for \(N=1000\), and compute the ratio of the storage figures.

Solution

At step \(k\), only the \(b\) rows below row \(k\) have a nonzero in column \(k\), and only the \(b\) columns to the right of column \(k\) have a nonzero in row \(k\). The step therefore forms \(b\) multipliers (\(b\) divisions) and updates a \(b\times b\) block with one multiplication and one subtraction per entry, \(2b^2\) flop. Near the end of the matrix fewer than \(b\) rows remain, which changes the count by \(O(b^3)\). Summing over the \(n\) steps gives \(W_{\text{band}}=2nb^2+O(nb+b^3)\). For \(n=N^2\) and \(b=N\) this is \(2N^4+O(N^3)\). The dense count is \(\tfrac23N^6\), so the ratio is \(2N^4\big/\tfrac23N^6=3/N^2\), which is \(3\times10^{-6}\) for \(N=1000\): dense elimination needs \(3.3\times10^{5}\) times more flop, the figure of the direct-cost section (\(6.7\times10^{17}\) against \(2\times10^{12}\)). The storage of the band factors is \(8n(2N+1)\) bytes and that of the dense matrix is \(8n^2\), so the ratio is \(n/(2N+1)\approx N/2=500\) for \(N=1000\), which agrees with 8 TB against 16 GB. In terms of the number of unknowns, \(n^3\) against \(n^2\) is the first two rows of Table 1.2.

(c) For \(T=86\,400\) s, compute the critical rate for the memory of Frontier, \(9.44\times10^{15}\) B, and for the 15 GB of the laptop of this chapter, and decide which constraint binds on each machine at its measured rate (\(1.206\times10^{18}\) flop/s and \(4\times10^{9}\) flop/s).

Solution

(a) From (1.1) and (1.3), the time limit is \(\tfrac23n^3\le RT\), so \(n_{\text{time}}=(1.5RT)^{1/3}\). From (1.4), the memory limit is \(8n^2\le M\), so \(n_{\text{mem}}=(M/8)^{1/2}\). (b) Storage binds first when \(n_{\text{mem}}\lt n_{\text{time}}\), that is \((M/8)^{1/2}\lt(1.5RT)^{1/3}\). Raising both sides to the power 3 gives \((M/8)^{3/2}\lt1.5RT\), which is \(R\gt(M/8)^{3/2}/(1.5T)\). (c) For Frontier, \(M/8=1.18\times10^{15}\), \((M/8)^{3/2}=4.05\times10^{22}\), and dividing by \(1.5\times86\,400=1.296\times10^{5}\) s gives \(3.13\times10^{17}\) flop/s. The measured \(R_{\max}=1.206\times10^{18}\) is 3.9 times larger, so storage binds: \(n_{\text{mem}}=3.44\times10^{7}\) and \(n_{\text{time}}=5.39\times10^{7}\). For the laptop, \(M/8=1.875\times10^{9}\), \((M/8)^{3/2}=8.12\times10^{13}\), and the critical rate is \(6.3\times10^{8}\) flop/s. The measured solve rates of 2.9 to 4.2 Gflop/s are 5 to 7 times larger, so storage binds there too: for \(R=4\times10^{9}\), \(n_{\text{mem}}=4.33\times10^{4}\) and \(n_{\text{time}}=8.03\times10^{4}\). A machine would reach the time limit first only if its rate were below the critical rate, which for these two machines is 3.9 times and 5 to 7 times below the measured rate. For dense elimination, the ratio of rate to memory of both machines puts the constraint on storage.

Exercise 3 (counterexample). The lecture presents iterative methods as faster than direct methods. Give a case with \(n=1000\) in which conjugate gradients cost more flop than LU factorization, and state which assumptions of the comparison the case violates.

Solution

Take \(A=Q\Lambda Q^{T}\) of order \(n=1000\), where \(Q\) is a random orthogonal matrix and the eigenvalues in \(\Lambda\) are spaced geometrically from 1 to \(10^{6}\), so that the condition number is \(\kappa=10^{6}\). One CG iteration needs a dense matrix-vector product, \(2n^2=2\times10^{6}\) flop, plus \(O(n)\) work. LU factorization needs \(\tfrac23n^3=6.67\times10^{8}\) flop, so CG is cheaper only if it needs fewer than \(n/3\approx333\) iterations. The bound (1.7) with \(\varepsilon=10^{-6}\) allows up to \(\lceil\tfrac12\sqrt{10^{6}}\ln(2\times10^{6})\rceil=7255\) iterations. The next listing runs CG with a random right-hand side and the stopping rule of the iteration listing, on this matrix and on a matrix with the same \(\kappa\) and evenly spaced eigenvalues. Its output is one sample run, since the iteration count depends slightly on the rounding of the BLAS in use.

"""Conjugate gradients on a dense symmetric positive definite matrix of order 1000 with condition number 1e6:
the iteration count depends on how the eigenvalues are spread, not only on their ratio."""
import sys
import numpy as np

n, TOL, CAP = 1000, 1e-6, 20000
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.standard_normal((n, n)))      # random orthogonal eigenvectors
b = rng.standard_normal(n)

def cg_iterations(A):
    """Iterations until the residual norm is below TOL times the norm of b, starting from zero."""
    r = b.copy(); p = r.copy(); rr = r @ r; nb = np.sqrt(rr); k = 0
    while np.sqrt(rr) > TOL * nb and k < CAP:
        Ap = A @ p; alpha = rr / (p @ Ap)
        r -= alpha * Ap
        rr_new = r @ r; p = r + (rr_new / rr) * p; rr = rr_new; k += 1
    return k

lu = 2.0 / 3.0 * n**3
print(f"LU: {lu:.3e} flop; break-even at {n / 3:.0f} CG iterations of 2n^2 flop each")
ok = True
for name, lam in (("evenly spaced", np.linspace(1.0, 1e6, n)), ("geometric", np.geomspace(1.0, 1e6, n))):
    A = (Q * lam) @ Q.T
    A = 0.5 * (A + A.T)
    k = cg_iterations(A)
    ok = ok and k < CAP
    print(f"{name:<14} kappa = {lam[-1] / lam[0]:.0e}: {k:5d} iterations, CG {2.0 * n * n * k:.3e} flop, CG/LU = {2.0 * n * n * k / lu:.2f}")
sys.exit(0 if ok else 1)

LU: 6.667e+08 flop; break-even at 333 CG iterations of 2n^2 flop each
evenly spaced  kappa = 1e+06:   202 iterations, CG 4.040e+08 flop, CG/LU = 0.61
geometric      kappa = 1e+06:  6267 iterations, CG 1.253e+10 flop, CG/LU = 18.80

With the geometric spectrum, CG needed 6267 iterations in the printed run, which is \(1.25\times10^{10}\) flop, 18.8 times the cost of LU. The spectrum matters: with the same \(\kappa\) and evenly spaced eigenvalues the same program needed 202 iterations, which costs 0.61 of LU, and CG wins. The run also shows the loss of finite termination: 6267 iterations exceed \(n=1000\), whereas Hestenes and Stiefel obtain the solution in at most \(n\) iterations in exact arithmetic, and rounding errors destroy that property (Shewchuk, p. 33). The condition number alone does not decide the cost. The case violates two assumptions behind the lecture's comparison. The matrix is dense, so one iteration costs \(2n^2\) and not \(O(n)\), and the eigenvalues are spread evenly in the logarithm, so the iteration count grows with the full width of the spectrum. Iterative methods win for sparse matrices with a moderate condition number, with clustered or evenly spread eigenvalues, or with a good preconditioner, which this chapter did not treat.

Exercise 4 (diagnose an assumption). A report estimates the time of the CG solve of the model problem at \(n=10^{6}\) on Frontier as \(W/R_{\max}=9.246\times10^{10}/1.206\times10^{18}=7.7\times10^{-8}\) s. Name the assumption of (1.1) that this estimate relies on, say why it fails here, and give a corrected figure with its remaining limits.

Solution

The estimate assumes that the machine sustains \(R_{\max}\), the rate of dense LU in the Linpack benchmark, during the CG run. The section on flop/s shows that Frontier sustains \(1.4054\times10^{16}\) flop/s on HPCG, a conjugate-gradient benchmark, which is 1/85.8 of \(R_{\max}\). The corrected figure is \(9.246\times10^{10}/1.4054\times10^{16}=6.6\times10^{-6}\) s, 86 times longer. Two limits remain. HPCG solves another problem (the page opened gives only its name), so the factor of 86 is evidence of a gap and not a measurement of this problem. And the corrected figure is still a lower bound: the model of this chapter has no communication cost, and 9 216 nodes share only \(10^{6}\) unknowns, about 108 each. A conjugate-gradient iteration needs two global sums, whose cost chapters 14 to 16 model with latency and bandwidth. The honest statement is that the arithmetic needs at least 6.6 microseconds on the whole machine, and that a problem of this size would not use the whole machine.

Exercise 5 (design judgment). A group must solve the two-dimensional model problem with \(N=10^{4}\) points per side (\(n=10^{8}\)) to the tolerance \(10^{-6}\) within one hour. Machine A sustains \(10^{12}\) flop/s at best and has 64 GB; machine B sustains \(10^{14}\) flop/s at best and has 1 TB. Use the formulas of this chapter to decide which of the four methods can run on which machine, and say what measurement the group should make before it commits.

Solution

Dense LU needs \(8n^2=8\times10^{16}\) B, which fits on neither machine. Band LU needs \(8n(2N+1)=1.6\times10^{13}\) B, 16 TB, which fits on neither (\(2N^4=2\times10^{16}\) flop would take 200 s on B if it fit). Jacobi needs \(1.6\) GB and, by (1.6), \(2.8\times10^{8}\) sweeps, so \(1.4\times10^{17}\) flop: \(1.4\times10^{5}\) s, 39 hours, on A, and 1400 s, 23 minutes, on B at the nominal rate. CG needs 4 GB and, by the bound (1.7), 46 188 iterations, so \(9.2\times10^{13}\) flop: 92 s on A and 0.92 s on B at the nominal rate. Only Jacobi on B and CG on both machines meet the deadline at the nominal rate. The nominal rate is optimistic: if the machines run these iterations at 1/86 of their best, as Frontier does on HPCG, CG takes \(7.9\times10^{3}\) s, 2.2 hours, on A, which misses the deadline, and 79 s on B, which meets it; Jacobi on B takes 33 hours. The break-even is an efficiency of \(92/3600=2.6\,\%\) of the nominal rate for CG on A. A recommendation: use CG, on B if B is available. If only A is available, run the same code at \(N=10^{3}\) (\(10^{3}\) times less work: \(9.2\times10^{10}\) against \(9.2\times10^{13}\) flop), measure its rate in flop/s from the iteration count, and commit to the full run only if the measured rate is at least 2.6 % of the best. The observed iteration counts of the previous listing are about a third of the bound, which gives a margin of about 3.

Exercise 6 (implementation and measurement). Write a program that times numpy.linalg.solve for orders 400, 800, 1600 and 2400, fits a power law \(t=cn^{a}\) by least squares in logarithms, predicts the time at \(n=3200\), and then measures that time. Compare the exponent with 3 and the prediction with the measurement, and explain any discrepancy.

Solution 
import time
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)

def best_time(n, reps):
    A = rng.standard_normal((n, n)); b = rng.standard_normal(n)
    best = 1e9
    for _ in range(reps):
        t0 = time.perf_counter(); np.linalg.solve(A, b); best = min(best, time.perf_counter() - t0)
    return best

sizes = [400, 800, 1600, 2400]
times = [best_time(n, 3) for n in sizes]
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(np.log(sizes), np.log(times), 1)
predicted = float(np.exp(intercept) * 3200 ** slope)
measured = best_time(3200, 2)
print("fitted exponent %.2f, predicted %.2f s, measured %.2f s" % (slope, predicted, measured))

The program was run on the laptop of this chapter in two groups of runs. In the first three runs, made while other processes used the machine, the best times at \(n=2400\) were 3.4 to 3.8 s, the fitted exponents were 3.07 to 3.13, and the predictions for \(n=3200\) were 8.7 to 9.6 s. These first runs used the same fit without the final measurement, so they did not measure the time at \(n=3200\). In three later runs of the program above, made when the machine was quieter, the best times at \(n=2400\) were 2.2 to 2.3 s, the fitted exponents were 2.98 to 3.00, the predictions were 5.25 to 5.44 s, and the measured times at \(n=3200\) were 4.94 to 5.31 s. The prediction was 2 % to 9 % above the measurement. Two results follow. First, the exponent is close to the 3 of (1.3) when the rate is stable. The rates \(\tfrac23n^3/t\) in the quiet runs were 3.9 to 4.1 Gflop/s at \(n=400\) and 4.0 to 4.2 at \(n=2400\). Second, in the busy runs the rate fell from 3.0 to 3.2 Gflop/s at \(n=400\) to 2.4 to 2.7 Gflop/s at \(n=2400\), which raised the exponent above 3 and the prediction above the time that the quiet machine delivers. The rate that the quiet machine delivered at \(n=2000\) in the listing of the section on flop/s, 3.6 to 4.1 Gflop/s, implies 5.3 to 6.1 s at \(n=3200\), which agrees with the quiet runs and not with the busy ones. A prediction of a run time from a fit is as reliable as the stability of the rate during the measurements. On a shared machine, the measurement of the time itself, at the size in question, is the safer figure.
